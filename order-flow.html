Order Flow for Beginners

A candle shows four prices. A footprint shows every executed trade inside it, at the bid and at the ask. Beginners drown trying to read every number. Read four things, and only at a level you already marked.

What is order flow?

Order flow is the record of every executed trade inside each candle, split into trades at the bid and at the ask. A candle only shows four prices, while a footprint chart shows volume at every price inside it: where buyers and sellers were active, where big players traded and who was more aggressive.

What order flow shows you

A standard candle only shows where it opened, where it closed, the high and the low. Order flow lets you see inside each candle: the trades that were actually executed at each price, split into the bid and the ask. The chart that shows it is called a footprint.

Inside one candle

1

Why it's worth learning

  • It shows how volume was distributed inside each candle: where buyers were active, where sellers were, and where nobody was.
  • It shows big player activity. Institutions trade huge size, and their heavy volume leaves footprints you can spot.
  • It shows who is dominating, buyers or sellers, and who is more aggressive.

Aggression equals direction. When buyers are the more aggressive side, price goes up.

2

Executed trades, not promises

  • Every number in a footprint is a trade that actually happened.
  • That's different from the order book (depth of market), which shows resting orders that may never fill.
  • Order flow isn't about reading all the numbers. It's about spotting a few specific things.

No other indicator shows volume per price inside each candle.

Who shows up where

The part that confuses most people. Every trade has a buyer and a seller; the footprint records which side crossed the spread.

BID · left

Aggressive sellers: market sell orders.

Passive buyers: limit buys getting filled.

A big number here at support can mean a big buyer waiting.
ASK · right

Aggressive buyers: market buy orders.

Passive sellers: limit sells getting filled.

A big number here at resistance can mean a big seller waiting.

You can't know for certain

A number on the ask could be an aggressive buyer or a passive seller. Context decides the best guess.

at a level you markedA reasonable read

What you need before you start

Order flow isn't part of most standard charting platforms. You need a platform that draws footprints, and a data feed that carries every executed trade.

1

A platform with footprint charts

  • NinjaTrader is a common choice for futures traders; its free version can run third-party order flow tools.
  • TradingView calls it the volume footprint and puts it on its higher paid plans. Fine to try if you already have that plan; more serious order flow traders tend to move on to dedicated tools.
  • Sierra Chart and other platforms have their own footprint charts. Any of them works for the basics.

The specific software matters less than reading the same four things on it every day.

2

Futures data, level 1

  • Order flow needs level 1 data: executed trades, with the bid and ask they traded at.
  • Level 2 (market depth) shows pending limit orders. Most never fill: algorithms often place them to lure price one way and pull them as price gets close.
  • You subscribe to the exchange that lists your instrument. One exchange's data costs a lot less than all of them.

Only level 1 is needed. Executed trades are what the footprint is built from.

Why futures, not spot forex

You need accurate volume at every price, and that needs a central exchange.

futures · centralizedEveryone sees the same data

One exchange: the same prices, the same volume, the same bid and ask split for every trader on the planet.

spot forex · decentralizedEach feed is a bit different

No central exchange, so volumes differ by provider and the bid and ask split is weak or missing. Read the currency future instead.

The four things to read

The biggest problem order flow traders have is that it shows so much: the chart is full of numbers and beginners don't know what to look at first. Four things make it far easier: large orders, volume clusters, delta and imbalances.

Four things, not every number

read 1

Large orders

The high volume node: the busiest price in each footprint, usually boxed by the software.

Footprints of the big players. Watch how it shifts from candle to candle.
read 2

Volume clusters

Heavy volume across several neighbouring prices, easiest to see with cells set to total volume.

Institutions spread big orders over many prices, so they show as blocks, not single cells.
read 3

Delta

Volume at the ask minus volume at the bid, printed under each footprint.

Positive: buyers in control. Negative: sellers. Price usually follows it.
read 4

Imbalances

One side trading about 3 times the other at a price, highlighted (often in blue).

Stacked on top of each other they show aggression. One alone means little.
Δ

Two ways to use delta

  • Divergence. Price rising with negative delta means sellers are dominating a rising market. Something fishy is going on, and price may turn from up to down, because price follows delta. Not always, but quite often.
  • At a level. Price reaches a resistance zone and prints a footprint with massively negative delta: somebody big is selling, quite possibly reacting to the same resistance. That's a confirmation to go short.

The same reads in reverse at support: price falling with positive delta, or a big positive delta at the level.

▦

Reading imbalances right

  • A selling imbalance shows on the bid: aggressive sellers. A buying imbalance shows on the ask: aggressive buyers.
  • A common default is 300%: one side at least 3 times the other. Many platforms compare diagonally (the bid at one price against the ask one tick higher); the idea is the same.
  • Stacked imbalances (three or more on top of each other) tell a story. A single one usually doesn't.

Selling imbalances cluster in downtrends, buying imbalances in uptrends.

Two cell settings

Most footprint tools can switch what each cell shows. Use both.

One number per price, shaded by size. Volume clusters jump out at a glance.

Use it as confirmation, not a strategy

The workflow: find a level, for example with a volume profile. Wait for price to pull back to it. Then open the footprint and look for confirmation that big players are reacting there. Without a level, order flow is just noise.

The level says where, the footprint says when

1

Mark the point of control

  • A volume profile is a histogram of volume at each price. Where it's widest, the most volume traded: the point of control.
  • Mark it. It's a strong level to trade from.
  • Price hits it from below: resistance, look for shorts. Price hits it from above: support, look for longs.

Support and resistance are zones, not exact prices. Look for the confirmation somewhere inside the zone.

2

Wait, then confirm

  • Don't trade the touch. Price can move around the level for a while without confirming anything.
  • When it reaches the level, open the footprint and wait for big players to show up: absorption, a big limit order, aggressive orders or a delta divergence.
  • When you see it, pull the trigger.

Confirmations also help when the level isn't especially strong, or price is driving into it fast.

Why the point of control holds

Two forces push price away when it comes back, and they push the same way.

reason 1The big players defend it

The heaviest volume traded there, so that's where institutions built positions. When price returns, they defend them.

reason 2The other side gets out

Traders on the wrong side don't want a fight with strong buyers or sellers. Closing a short means buying, which adds to the push.

The absorption setup

The favorite confirmation in the video. Price drops into a strong support, for example a point of control. At the level you want to see unusually heavy volume on both the bid and the ask: sellers pushing, buyers taking everything they sell.

514 at the bid, 1,025 at the ask

1

Identify the zone first

  • Use your main method: a volume profile point of control, a heavy-volume level, price action.
  • Only when price gets near that zone do you open the footprint chart.

The absorption has to appear around the zone. Anywhere else it doesn't count.

2

Wait for heavy volume on both sides

  • Unusually heavy volume on the bid (sellers still pushing) and on the ask (buyers taking it all) at the same price.
  • Price stops dropping and stays at one level. The market is absorbing the selling pressure.
  • It can take a few minutes for the orders to build before you can tell it's absorption.

A positive delta on that footprint is a bonus: buyers are jumping in.

3

Enter as soon as you see it

  • Long from support, short from resistance (the mirror image: buying pressure absorbed at resistance).
  • Stop beyond the zone you're trading.
  • A 5-minute or 30-minute footprint works well for spotting it.

Level first, absorption second. Never the other way round.

What "unusually heavy" means

It's different for every instrument and every session, which is one reason to start with just one market.

Four confirmations at a level

From the book that goes with the video. These aren't setups on their own: their strength is telling you the market is starting to react to a support or resistance zone you already found.

Four ways big players show up

1

Big limit order

  • Someone big waits at the zone and fills passively with a limit order.
  • Support: an unusually large number on the bid (a limit buy). Resistance: on the ask (a limit sell).
  • It may take a few minutes for the whole order to fill. A 5-minute bid × ask chart works well.

Enter as soon as you can identify it.

2

Absorption

  • Heavy volume on both the bid and the ask around the zone.
  • The momentum that brought price there is being soaked up, making a reversal likely.

Simpler than the limit order read: you just need both sides heavy.

3

Aggressive orders and delta

  • In resistance, big volume starts appearing on the bid: aggressive sellers hitting it with market orders because they don't want to miss the trade.
  • At support, the ask. It shows up in the delta too: negative for a short, positive for a long.

They see the same level you do, and they're paying up to trade it.

4

Cumulative delta divergence

  • Cumulative delta is the running total of every footprint's delta since the start of the day.
  • Put a 1-minute price chart above a 1-minute cumulative delta line. For a long, price falls into support while cumulative delta rises. For a short, price rises into resistance while cumulative delta falls.

Easy to read, which is why it's popular.

Where the levels come from

The book also gives standalone order flow setups that build levels on the 30-minute footprint, plus volume profile setups for the bigger picture. Each one marks a zone; you trade the pullback to it.

order flow · 1

Volume clusters

A heavy block formed in a trend, or inside a strong rejection of higher or lower prices.

Enter at the start of the cluster or its heaviest price. Formed in an uptrend: long.
order flow · 2

Multiple nodes

Two or more footprints in a row with their high volume node at the same price.

In a trend, before one, or in a strong rejection. More nodes, stronger level.
order flow · 3

Big trades only

A filter that hides everything except single trades above a size you set.

Set it so you get roughly 5 to 10 signals a day. The color doesn't decide the side.
order flow · 4

Stacked imbalances

A zone of stacked buying imbalances (support) or selling imbalances (resistance).

Best within a trend or before one; a strong one-way move will do.
bonus

Unfinished business

A high or low where both the bid and ask were above zero: a failed auction.

Works like a magnet. Use it to stretch a target or to avoid a bad entry, not as a setup.
volume profile · 1

Accumulation

A tight rotation, then a strong trend out of it. The heaviest volume in the rotation is the level.

Institutions build size quietly in a rotation before they move it.
volume profile · 2

Trend clusters

In a strong trend the profile is thin. A small bump of volume inside it is the level.

Trade with the trend: longs in an uptrend, shorts in a downtrend.
volume profile · 3

Rejection

A strong, aggressive rejection of higher or lower prices. Its heaviest volume is the level.

The hardest of the three: the rejection has to be clearly aggressive.
↻

Rules the order flow setups share

  • Price must first move away: at least one or two whole footprints (30-minute) completely above or below the zone.
  • Then wait for the pullback. Enter when price returns to the zone.
  • Hit from above: long. Hit from below: short.
  • Trade only the first touch. The odds of a second good reaction are lower.

Volume clusters and multiple nodes don't need the bid × ask split, so they can be read on total volume.

Managing the trade with order flow

The same reads that get you in can tell you where to take profit, when to keep trailing, and where the stop makes sense.

take profitJust before heavy volume

Heavy-volume areas act as support and resistance, so take profit a little before the next one. Price often reacts slightly early. If the nearest one is too close for the reward, skip the trade or aim for the one after.

trailWhile imbalances are on your side

Long: keep trailing while buying imbalances keep printing. Stop trailing on a warning signal: any confirmation that points the other way (a limit order, absorption, aggressive orders or a delta divergence against you).

stop lossBehind the level, sized sensibly

A fixed stop, beyond the high or low of the zone, or in the low-volume area behind a heavy-volume area. Keep it roughly 10 to 20% of the average daily range (ATR); outside that, skip the trade or use a fixed stop.

A beginner's action plan

The video's quick plan for getting started, one step at a time.

1

Get a footprint chart

  • Choose a platform that draws footprints. A free futures platform with an order flow add-on is enough to learn on.
  • Set it up with bid × ask cells, delta under each footprint, and a way to switch to total volume.

Don't pay for features you won't read. Four things is the whole job at first.

2

Connect futures data

  • Level 1 data from the exchange that lists your instrument: executed trades with the bid and ask.
  • Skip level 2. Pending orders aren't what a footprint reads.

Futures: one central exchange, so your volume is everyone's volume.

3

Pick one instrument

  • Only one. Every instrument has different volume, volatility and active hours.
  • What counts as "unusually large" volume is different on each one, and you need to know it by feel.

Even the best traders trade one or two instruments. No more.

4

Week one: just observe

  • Watch large volumes, volume clusters, delta and imbalances on your one market.
  • Don't place any trades yet. Get to know the market and the footprint.

Learn what normal looks like first, so unusual stands out.

5

Add the point of control

  • Start combining with a volume profile and mark the point of control.
  • When price reaches it, open the footprint and look for confirmation, like absorption. Then trade.

Level first, order flow second: that's the whole method.

Questions

What does the bid and ask side of a footprint mean?

The bid side is aggressive sellers hitting market orders or passive limit buyers getting filled. The ask side is aggressive buyers or passive limit sellers. You can't know which for certain, so context decides the best guess.

What do you need to read order flow?

A platform that draws footprint charts, such as NinjaTrader, TradingView's volume footprint or Sierra Chart, and level 1 data for one exchange: executed trades with the bid and ask they traded at.

Why futures and not spot forex?

Footprints need accurate volume at every price, which needs a central exchange. Futures have one, so everyone sees the same data. Spot forex doesn't, so read the currency future instead.

Where should a beginner read order flow?

Only at a level you already marked. A big print in the middle of nowhere is a guess; heavy prints right at support or resistance are a reasonable read.

Without a level, order flow is just noise.

Find the level first, with a volume profile point of control or your own method. Wait for price to come back to it. Then let the footprint tell you whether big players are reacting there, and only then pull the trigger.

  • Read four things: large orders, volume clusters, delta, imbalances.
  • Futures and level 1 data: executed trades from one central exchange.
  • One instrument. A week of watching before the first trade.
  • Absorption: unusually heavy volume on the bid and the ask, at the level.
  • Take profit before the next heavy-volume area; trail only while the aggression is on your side.
