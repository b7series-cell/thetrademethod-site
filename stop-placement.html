Where Your Stop Belongs

Traders park the stop at the low of the day because they hate getting shaken out. Jeff Holden of SMB Capital says that's the wrong question. The right one: what would price have to do to prove this trade has changed?

You're asking the wrong question

The impulse makes sense. Nobody wants to be right about the stock and wrong about the timing, and nobody wants to get shaken out of a trade that was about to work. But a stop that's far away because you hate getting shaken out is answering a question about your feelings, not about the trade.

?

One question decides the stop

  • Not: how do I avoid being wrong?
  • Instead: what would price have to do to prove this trade has changed?
  • For a quick scalp off a defined level, that answer is usually much closer than the low of the day.

How far away the stop is and how it feels are secondary. The stop has one job: mark the price where the trade's claim is dead.

Two ways to pick a stop

the nerves stop"Give it room"

The low of the day, picked so you don't get shaken out. Comfortable, and comfort has a price, paid in reward to risk.

the thesis stop"Where it's proven wrong"

The price where the idea behind this trade stops being true. It belongs to the trade, not to your nerves.

What an opening range break claims

Every trade makes a claim. An opening range break's claim isn't "price goes up eventually". It's that price leaves the range and stays out. So the stop goes where that claim stops being true.

The claim, and where it dies

1

The stop has one job

  • Mark the price where the claim is dead.
  • For an opening range break: price back inside the range, and below VWAP, means it didn't leave and stay out.
  • In a choppy opening range, a failed break tends to run all the way to the other side, fast.

That's why a closer stop here doesn't cost you much probability. If the break fails, it's usually headed back down anyway.

The question isn't how do I avoid being wrong. It's what would price have to do to prove this trade has changed? — Jeff Holden, SMB Capital

Claims for other trades

Opening range break

Price leaves the range and stays out. Dead when it's back in the range and under VWAP.

Turn trade at support

Buyers defend the level right away. Dead the moment the level breaks, or price just sits there.

What a wide stop really costs

The video starts with a hypothetical, not a desk trade: buy at $50.00, target $50.60, stop at the session low of $49.40. That's 60 cents of risk for 60 cents of reward, one to one at best. The trade has to win more than half the time just to break even, and it was supposed to be a quick scalp off a defined level.

2

Wide doesn't mean safe

  • The wide stop didn't make the trade safer. It made the trade need to be right more often.
  • Cut that stop in half (49.70) and the same 60-cent target is two to one. You now break even winning one trade in three.
  • The cost of the closer stop: if price fails, it'll come lower quickly and likely stop you out. That's the point.

Low-of-day stops are a common thing to teach. The video's point isn't that they're always wrong, it's that the stop should come from the trade's structure, not from the fear of a shake-out.

A real review: same trade, different stop

From one of SMB's trade reviews: a trader took an opening range break in CoreWeave (CRWV). He waited for the pre-market high to break, went long around 57.82 as it broke out of the range, and targeted $60, which he called resistance. It broke 60, he sold 90% and kept a runner. A good trade. The entry was right, the target was right, the structure was right. The stop was the problem.

Ask the two questions

  • If price got to the low of the day, would the thesis still be right? No.
  • If price got back below VWAP, would the thesis still be right? Also no.
  • And if it fell just below 56, the odds it keeps going to 52 are pretty high. If it comes all the way back to VWAP and works anyway, maybe you got lucky, but it's not the same trade.

Same entry, same target, same probability. Only the stop moves, and the expected value over time gets a lot better.

The honest answer most traders give

"I hate for my thesis to be right and for me to get shaken out. I like to give it a lot of breathing room." That's the real conversation: what's breathing room, and what's just trading fearfully?

breathing roomRoom the trade needs

Space for normal noise before the claim is proven wrong.

fearRoom your nerves want

Space past the point where the trade has already failed.

Start wide if you have to, then move it

You can start with your stop far away on entry. That's what this trader wanted. His mistake was not moving it up as the trade went his way. As soon as the range is broken, ask where this trade fails, and if it does, how likely it is to go a lot lower.

Am I wrong if it drops this?

4

Have the conversation with yourself

  • If it drops VWAP, am I wrong?
  • If it drops the pre-market high, am I wrong?
  • If it drops the opening price, am I wrong?
  • Each "yes" is a level the stop can move up to.

Experienced traders have this conversation all the time. Keep the low-of-day stop if you want, but then you have to manage the risk, not just the reward.

Why moving it up protects the probability

In a choppy opening range, a break that fails has a really high chance of going all the way back to the other side, fast.

stop under VWAPSmall loss, same odds

If it fails, it was going back anyway. You're out with a fraction of the risk.

stop at the lowFull loss, same odds

You take the whole trip down to the low and call it breathing room.

Same stock, same morning, a different trade

Which would you rather take: the breakout at 57.82, or the bounce at 54? A second trader wasn't looking at the opening range break at all. He took the first pullback, a turn trade, when CRWV pulled down to $54: higher time frame support, the low of the range, where the pre-market buyers were. He saw buyers step in with conviction and went long.

The turn trade at 54

5

A tight stop that belongs

  • The stop sat just under 54. If 54 breaks, the level is gone, and the trade is gone with it.
  • The best turn trades risk 10 or 20 cents to make a dollar.
  • He took pieces off back toward the top of the range, with a resting order at 56.50 for the last one.
  • If it drops 54, you're out. If it dilly-dallies and bounces around, you don't have a trade that's going to give you continuation either.

Don't guess and don't second-guess. The thesis is a strong stock pulling into a key level where buyers step in. That's the stop.

A closer stop isn't free

Let's be honest about the other side. A closer stop will trigger more exits, and one that's too close will trigger far too many, including trades that would have recovered. That can't be settled by one example. Before a tighter stop becomes a rule, check it against your own trades.

6

Let your sample decide

  • Pull your own breakouts and your own turn trades.
  • How often did the closer stop exit?
  • What was the average loss?
  • What did the wider stop cost when it finally hit?

A stop that exits early and small can be the better stop on some trades. A turn trade needs a tight one. An opening range break wants neither too tight nor too loose: balance the probability against the reward to risk.

Write the invalidation down before you enter

Before your next breakout, write one line: the price behavior that proves this trade wrong. Not the dollar amount you're willing to lose. The behavior. If you can't write it, you don't actually have a stop. You have a hope, and hope is not a risk plan.

7

Write it, trade it, review it

  • Write the invalidation before the entry.
  • Trade the plan.
  • Review the samples.

That's how a stop becomes a system instead of a feeling.

A billion-dollar lesson in thesis discipline

Jeff closes with a story he learned early in his career. It's the same discipline at a completely different scale: when the thesis changes, you're out.

January 2022

The position

Bill Ackman's Pershing Square buys about 3.1 million shares of Netflix, roughly a $1.1 billion position.

April 2022

The thesis changes

Netflix reports its first subscriber loss in about a decade, and management pivots to an ad-supported plan it had ruled out before.

Days later

The exit

He sells the entire position at a loss of around $400 million, saying he'd lost confidence in his ability to predict the company's future.

The lesson

No hold and hope

He didn't wait for the old thesis to come back. You don't have to pay $400 million to practice the same thing: a stop tied to the trade you took.

Your stop, step by step

The whole idea as a routine for every trade. Click through it.

1

Name the claim

  • What is this trade saying will happen? Not "it goes up". Be specific.
  • Opening range break: it leaves the range and stays out. Turn trade: buyers defend the level right away.

Different trades make different claims, so they get different stops, even on the same stock the same morning.

2

Write what proves it wrong

  • One line, before the entry: the price behavior that kills the claim.
  • Behavior, not a dollar amount.

If you can't write it, you don't have a stop. You have a hope.

3

Put the stop there

  • Just past where that behavior shows up: under VWAP, under the range, under the level.
  • Check the reward to risk. Same target, a closer honest stop, a better trade.

Not too tight to survive normal noise, not so wide that it sits past the point the trade has already failed.

4

Move it as it works

  • Started wide? Ask "am I wrong if it drops this?" at each level: VWAP, the pre-market high, the opening price.
  • Each yes is a new place for the stop.

You make the trade better without making it any harder to be right.

5

Let the sample decide

  • Track how often the closer stop exits, the average loss, and what the wide stop cost when it hit.
  • Only then turn the observation into a rule.

That's how a stop becomes a system instead of a feeling.

Questions

Why is a low-of-day stop often wrong for a breakout?

It sits far past the point where the breakout has already failed. On the CRWV trade in the video it risked about 3.82 to make 2.18, so the trade had to win about 64% of the time just to break even.

Does a closer stop lower the win rate?

A little, sometimes. In a choppy opening range a failed break usually runs back to the other side fast, so a stop under VWAP barely changes the odds while cutting the risk roughly in half.

Can you start with a wide stop?

Yes, but move it as the trade works. Ask at each level, like VWAP, the pre-market high or the opening price, whether losing it would make the trade wrong. If yes, the stop goes there.

What is a turn trade stop?

A stop just under the level you bought. The thesis is that buyers defend that level right away, so if it breaks or price just sits there, you're out. The best turn trades risk 10 to 20 cents to make a dollar.

How do you know if a tighter stop is better?

Test it on your own trades first: how often the closer stop got hit, the average loss, and what the wider stop cost when it was hit. Let the sample decide before it becomes a rule.

A stop belongs to the trade, not to your nerves.

Ask what price would have to do to prove the trade has changed, and put the stop there. Write it down before you enter, move it as the trade works, and let your own sample tell you when a tighter stop should become a rule.

  • Name the trade's claim.
  • Write the behavior that proves it wrong.
  • Put the stop where that happens.
  • Started wide? Move it up at each "am I wrong?" level.
  • Turn trades: risk cents under the level.
  • Check closer stops against your own sample first.
