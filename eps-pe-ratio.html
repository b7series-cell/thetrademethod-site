EPS and the P/E Ratio

Own a share and you own a slice of the company's profit. Earnings per share is the size of that slice. The P/E ratio is what investors are paying for it. Neither number means much without context.

What are EPS and the P/E ratio?

Earnings per share (EPS) is a company's net income, minus preferred dividends, divided by its shares outstanding: the profit each share earns. The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is the share price divided by EPS: how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of yearly earnings.

Earnings per share

Owning a share makes you a legal part owner of the company, and part owners get a portion of the profits. EPS measures that portion.

1

The formula

  • Net income: the total profit after all expenses and taxes.
  • Minus preferred dividends: preferred shareholders get a fixed dividend first (and usually no vote), so that money isn't available to common shareholders.
  • Divided by shares outstanding: the common shares in the market.

EPS = (net income − preferred dividends) ÷ shares outstanding. Sites like Yahoo Finance show it, but it isn't always up to date, especially for smaller companies, so check it against other sources.

Cutting the cake

2

Two things move EPS

  • Profit: more sales, lower costs or better efficiency raise EPS. Less net income lowers it.
  • Share count: more shares split the same profit into smaller slices; fewer shares make each slice bigger.

The birthday cake: more guests arrive, so you cut more, smaller slices. If almost nobody wants cake, the few who do get bigger ones.

Dilution and buybacks

more sharesDilution

A company issues new shares for quick cash, to pay debt or buy another company. Each share gets a smaller slice of profit.

fewer sharesBuybacks

A company buys its own shares back. The rest get a bigger slice, and EPS rises, which some companies do to keep investors happy.

When EPS is negative

A net loss gives a negative EPS. Startups and growing companies often spend heavily before they turn a profit. It shows financial struggle, but not automatically a bad investment: look at why, and at revenue growth, cash flow and debt.

The price-to-earnings ratio

"Is this stock overvalued or not?" is the most common question in value investing. The P/E ratio is one quick way to start answering it: share price on top, earnings per share on the bottom.

What a P/E says, and what it doesn't

3

Dollars paid per dollar earned

  • A stock at $100 with $10 of EPS has a P/E of 10: investors pay $10 for every $1 the company earns in a year.
  • If earnings stayed the same, it would take about 10 years of earnings to make back the price.
  • A high P/E can mean investors expect fast growth. A low one can mean the stock is undervalued, or the company is facing trouble.

Think of it as a measure of the market's expectations, not a verdict.

A P/E only means something next to its industry

Different industries carry different growth, risk and business models, so their normal P/E ranges differ. Comparing a company with its industry average is far more useful than looking at one P/E alone.

high P/E

Growth industries

Technology, biotech, e-commerce. Investors pay more for today's earnings because they expect fast growth.

A tech startup with new products might sit at 50 or more.
low P/E

Mature or cyclical

Utilities, manufacturing, energy. Slower growth, or earnings that swing with the economy.

Investors won't pay a high premium. Consumer goods sit in between.
no P/E

Early-stage startups or heavily indebted companies that aren't profitable yet.

4

Trailing vs forward

  • Trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of earnings: real data.
  • Forward P/E uses analysts' estimate for the next 12 months: a guess about where the company is heading.
  • Forward lower than trailing: earnings are expected to grow. Forward higher: they're expected to shrink.

BrightTech at $100: last year's EPS of $5 gives a trailing P/E of 20. An expected $10 gives a forward P/E of 10. If the estimate were $4, it would be 25. Estimates can be wrong.

Questions

How do you calculate earnings per share?

Subtract preferred dividends from net income, then divide by the common shares outstanding. A company with $5,000,000 of net income, $200,000 of preferred dividends and 1,000,000 shares has an EPS of $4.80.

What makes EPS go up or down?

Profit and the share count. Higher net income raises EPS. Issuing new shares (dilution) lowers it, and buying shares back raises it, even with no change in profit.

What is a good P/E ratio?

There isn't one number. P/E ranges differ by industry: growth industries like tech often run high, mature or cyclical ones like utilities run low. Compare a company with its own industry's average.

What is the difference between trailing and forward P/E?

Trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of actual earnings. Forward P/E uses analysts' estimates for the next 12 months. A forward P/E below the trailing one means earnings are expected to grow.

Can EPS be negative?

Yes, when a company has a net loss. Then the P/E isn't meaningful. It doesn't automatically mean a bad investment: look at why, and at revenue growth, cash flow and debt.

Does a low P/E mean a stock is cheap?

Not always. It can mean the stock is undervalued, or that the company is facing problems. A high P/E can mean expected growth rather than overvaluation.

Two numbers, never alone.

EPS tells you how much profit each share earns. P/E tells you how much the market pays for it. Both are quick, useful starting points, and both can mislead without the industry, the share count, debt and cash flow next to them.

  • EPS = (net income − preferred dividends) ÷ shares.
  • More shares dilute it; buybacks lift it.
  • P/E = share price ÷ EPS.
  • Compare P/E within the same industry.
  • Trailing is real; forward is an estimate.
  • Low isn't always cheap; high isn't always expensive.
