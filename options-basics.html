How do options work?

An option is the right, not the obligation, to buy (a call) or sell (a put) 100 shares at a fixed price, the strike, until a deadline, the expiration. The buyer pays a premium and can lose no more than that. The seller keeps the premium and takes on the obligation, with much larger risk.

The house deposit

You want a house but aren't ready to commit. You pay the seller a $2,000 non-refundable deposit for the right to buy it at today's price any time in the next 3 months. If it gets more valuable, you still buy at the locked price. If you change your mind, you walk away and lose only the $2,000.

1

You're buying a right, not the thing

  • You don't buy the asset. You buy the right to buy or sell it later at a price fixed today.
  • A call is the right to buy at the strike price.
  • A put is the right to sell at the strike price.

That's the whole concept. Everything else is vocabulary.

The vocabulary, on the video's example

A fictional company trading at $100 a share.

Premium

The price of the option itself, the deposit. Here $2 per share.

Contract

Options come in contracts of 100 shares, so one costs $2 × 100 = $200.

Strike price

The price you've locked in to buy or sell at later. Here $100.

Expiration

The deadline. After it, an unused option is worthless. Here one month.

Anna buys a call, Marco sells it

Anna pays $200 for one call: strike $100, one month. Every option has two sides. The person who sold it to her, Marco, is often in real life an institution, a market maker, or someone running an income strategy.

One call, both sides

2

Three things can happen

  • Up to $120: she can buy 100 shares at $100 that are worth $120. $2,000 minus the $200 premium is $1,800 on a $200 investment.
  • Flat at $100, or down to $95: no reason to buy at $100. She lets it expire and loses $200.
  • Crash to $50: the same. Still $200. Her risk was fixed the moment she bought.

Gain with no set ceiling, loss fixed and known in advance. That asymmetry is the whole appeal of buying a call.

The mirror image

Anna, the buyerPays $200, has a right

Most she can lose: $200. Most she can make: no set limit if the stock keeps rising.

Marco, the sellerKeeps $200, has an obligation

If Anna exercises, he must sell 100 shares at $100. Above $102 he loses, and in theory there's no limit.

If Marco didn't own the shares and the stock is at $120, he buys at $120 to deliver at $100: $2,000 minus the $200 he kept, a $1,800 loss.

Puts are the same thing, flipped

A put is the right to sell at the strike price: useful when you think a stock will fall, or to protect shares you already own.

3

Anna buys a $100 put for $200

  • The stock drops to $70: she can sell at $100 shares worth $70. $3,000, minus $200, is $2,800.
  • The stock rises or stays flat: she lets it expire and loses the $200.
  • Marco, who sold the put, keeps the $200, but has to buy at $100 if the stock falls, and loses more the further it falls.

Put buyers have capped risk and a large reward if prices fall. Put sellers have a capped reward and growing risk as prices fall.

The two forces nobody teaches until they cost you

Everything so far is about where the stock ends up. But two more forces act on every option every day: time, and how nervous the market is.

Time decay and the volatility crush

4

Right about the direction, still losing

  • Time decay: part of every premium is time value, paid for the chance the stock moves. It shrinks every day, and faster near expiration.
  • Implied volatility: when investors are scared, around earnings, uncertainty or crashes, options cost more, like insurance in a hurricane zone.
  • When the fear goes away, the price drops even if the stock doesn't: the volatility crush.

Many first-time buyers call the direction right, watch the stock do nothing for a few weeks, and lose most of their money to time before the move arrives.

How Marco sells first and buys later

  • When Marco sells the option, the market hands him $200 and his account shows minus one contract: a debt.
  • Option A: buy it back at $80 now. The debt is gone and he keeps $120. Trade closed.
  • Option B: wait weeks for expiration and keep all $200 if the stock stays flat, while a bad headline could still send the stock flying.

He sold high first and bought low second. Leaving a trade open to squeeze out the last $80 is a risk choice, not free money.

Most traders sell the ticket, not use it

Textbooks say Anna will use her call to buy 100 shares. In the real world almost nobody does. They buy the contract, wait for it to gain value, then sell the contract itself back to the market.

5

The concert ticket

  • You buy a $100 ticket to a sold-out show. The band blows up and the same ticket now sells for $500.
  • You can go to the concert, or resell the ticket for a $400 profit.
  • In the options market, almost everyone resells the ticket.

Exercising early throws away the time value still in the price. Selling the contract captures all of it.

When exercising can make sense

  • You really want to own the stock for the next 10 years.
  • A big dividend is coming that's worth more than the time left on the option.
  • The option is illiquid and you can't sell it at a fair price.

That dividend case is also the seller's hidden risk: most US stock options are American style, so the buyer can exercise any time, and the seller can be assigned with little warning.

Three real uses

Options get called gambling. That describes how some people use them, not what they are.

protection

Hedging

Own shares, worried about a short-term drop? Buy a put as insurance.

If the stock falls, the put's gains offset the shares' losses. If not, you lose the premium.
income

Covered calls

Own shares, sell calls against them, and collect the premium, with time decay on your side.

If the stock rises past the strike you may have to sell, but you keep the premium. Read the page ›
leverage

Speculation

A strong view, a small amount of capital, a large position, like Anna's call.

It amplifies the return, and the chance of losing the whole premium, especially if the move is late.

Before your first option trade

Five things to know before you buy or sell a contract. Click through them.

1

Work out the break-even

  • Call: strike plus the premium per share. Put: strike minus the premium.
  • That's where the stock has to be at expiration just to get your money back.

The premium isn't just a fee. It's a hurdle.

2

Know the most you can lose

  • Buying: the premium, and many options do expire worthless.
  • Selling: frequent small wins, with the chance of a large, sudden loss.

Start with an amount you're genuinely okay with losing.

3

Count the time you have

  • How long until expiration, and does your idea need longer than that?
  • Decay speeds up in the final days and weeks.

Being right too late pays the same as being wrong.

4

Check how scared the market is

  • Before earnings or in a panic, options are expensive.
  • After the news, that extra can vanish overnight.

A buyer can lose money even when the stock doesn't move against them.

5

Plan the exit

  • Most traders sell the contract to close, rather than exercise.
  • Sellers: know you can be assigned early, and when you'd buy back.

You're trading a flexible piece of paper that moves with the stock.

Questions

What is the difference between a call and a put?

A call gives you the right to buy at the strike price, so it gains when the stock rises. A put gives you the right to sell at the strike price, so it gains when the stock falls.

How do you calculate an option's break-even?

For a call, add the premium per share to the strike: a $100 call bought for $2 breaks even at $102. For a put, subtract it: a $100 put bought for $2 breaks even at $98.

What does in the money mean?

A call is in the money when the stock is above the strike, and a put when it's below. In the money isn't the same as profitable: a $100 call at $101 is worth $100 per contract, but if you paid $200 you're still down $100.

What is time decay?

Part of an option's price is time value, paid for the chance the stock moves before expiration. It shrinks every day, faster near expiration, so an option can lose value even when the stock doesn't move.

What is a volatility crush?

Options get expensive when the market is nervous, like before earnings. After the news, the fear disappears and the option's price can drop sharply even if the stock barely moves. In the video, a $200 call fell to $80.

Do you have to exercise an option to make money?

No. Most traders sell the contract itself back to the market. Exercising early throws away the time value still in the price.

Powerful, and completely unforgiving.

The video's own advice: if you're watching because you're curious how options work, great. If you're about to open an account and start trading them, don't, or at least start with an amount you're genuinely okay with losing.
