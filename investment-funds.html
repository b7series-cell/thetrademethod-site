Every Fund, Three Questions

Index funds, ETFs, mutual funds, hedge funds: there are dozens of names. Almost every one is explained by three questions: how you buy it, who decides what it buys, and what's inside. Then check what it charges every year.

What are the main types of investment funds?

Almost every fund is explained by three questions. How you buy it: a mutual fund from the fund company once a day, an ETF on the stock exchange, a closed-end fund from other investors, or a private fund like a hedge fund. What a fund is

Picture a group of friends who each put $100 into one shared shopping cart, and one person goes to the store and buys for everyone. A fund works the same way: thousands of people pool their money, and the fund buys many investments at once.

1

One purchase, a piece of everything

  • The fund uses the pooled money to buy stocks, bonds or other investments.
  • With a single purchase, you own a small piece of everything inside.
  • That spread is called diversification, and it's why funds are one of the easiest ways to start investing.

The person doing the shopping doesn't work for free. Every fund charges a yearly fee called the expense ratio, a small percentage of your money. It matters more than it looks.

The three questions

1. How do you buy it?

The package the fund comes in: mutual fund, ETF, closed-end fund, or a private fund most people can't buy.

2. Who decides what it buys?

A manager picking investments (active), or nobody, because the fund copies a list (index).

3. What's inside?

From calm, like short-term loans, to nervous, like a single sector or a leveraged ETF.

Question 1: how do you buy it?

The same kind of fund can come in different packages. The package decides when you can buy, at what price, and who can get in at all.

the classic

Mutual fund

Bought from the fund company, not on the stock exchange. Priced once a day after the close.

Often a minimum first investment; easy automatic monthly buys.
in broker apps

ETF

An exchange-traded fund. Bought and sold on the exchange like one stock, any time the market is open.

One share, or less with fractional shares, is enough to start.
less common

Closed-end fund

Raises money once, then never creates or cancels shares. You buy from another investor.

The price can sit above or below what the holdings are worth.
private

Hedge, private equity, venture

Hedge funds have very few limits. Private equity buys whole companies; venture capital backs startups.

Usually for accredited investors only, and the money is locked up.
2

Mutual funds price once a day

  • The price is the net asset value (NAV): everything the fund owns, divided by its number of shares.
  • It's worked out once a day, after the market closes.
  • Order during the day and you don't know your exact price yet. You get that day's NAV, or the next day's if you're past the fund's daily deadline.

An ETF trades all day at whatever the market pays. Being able to trade any minute doesn't mean you should: many long-term investors buy ETFs and simply leave them alone.

A closed-end fund can cost more than it's worth

Who can buy a hedge fund?

  • Usually an accredited investor in the US: a net worth over $1 million not counting your home, or income over $200,000 a year.
  • Traditionally they charge 2% of your money every year plus 20% of the profits, although many charge less today.
  • They can borrow to invest more, bet on falling prices and invest in almost anything.

Question 2: who decides what it buys?

In an actively managed fund, a professional manager or a team picks the investments, trying to beat the market. In an index fund, nobody picks anything: the fund copies a list of companies called an index.

3

Index or active

  • An index fund copies a list like the S&P 500, around 500 of the largest US companies.
  • An active fund pays a manager to choose, and charges more for it.
  • Do active managers beat the market? Usually not. In a typical year, around two out of three active funds that invest in large US companies do worse than the S&P 500.

That doesn't make active funds always a bad idea. It explains why index funds became so popular.

An index fund is not a package

This clears up the most common confusion. "Index" answers question 2, not question 1, so an index fund can come either way.

S&P 500 index ETFBought on the exchange

Trades all day like a stock.

S&P 500 index mutual fundBought from the fund company

Priced once a day at NAV.

Both own basically the same companies. The big difference is how you buy them.

What the yearly fee really costs

Index funds often charge less than 0.15% a year, stock mutual funds around 0.4% on average, and some active funds 1% or more. It sounds tiny, but it's charged every single year on all your money, whether the fund makes money or not.

4

The only thing that changed was the fee

  • $10,000 for 30 years, growing 7% a year before fees.
  • At 0.05% a year you'd end with around $75,000.
  • At 1% a year, around $57,000. About $18,000 less.

Question 3: what's inside?

This is probably the most important question. From the calmest funds to the most nervous ones.

calmest

Money market fund

Loans that last days or months, mostly to governments and big companies. Price designed to stay at $1; it pays interest.

Used to park cash. Not a bank account: not government-insured, and in rare cases it can lose value.
calm

Bond fund

Loans to governments or companies that pay interest, usually for several years. Calmer than stocks.

Weak spot: when rates rise, older bonds fall. Long-term bond funds can swing almost like stocks.
owns companies

Dividend fund

Companies that share part of their profit with shareholders every year. Popular for regular income.

Be careful with funds chasing the highest dividends: a very high one can signal trouble.
one basket

Sector and thematic

One area only: technology, healthcare, clean energy.

Can grow fast while popular, but trends go out of fashion as quickly as they came.
buildings

Real estate fund

Usually REITs: companies that own offices, malls, apartments or warehouses and collect rent.

A share of the rent without buying a building. Prices move a lot when rates change.
raw materials

Commodity fund

Gold, oil and other raw materials. Some use gold as protection when markets get scary.

A bar of gold pays no rent or dividends: you only make money if someone later pays more.
5

Why bond funds fall when rates rise

  • Your old bond pays 3%. New bonds start paying 5%.
  • Nobody will pay full price for yours. To sell it, you'd have to lower the price.
  • How much a bond fund moves depends on how long its bonds last: short-term barely moves, 20 to 30 year bonds can swing almost like stocks.

Two special designs

less risk over timeTarget-date fund

A year in the name, usually when you plan to retire. Mostly stocks when you're young, sliding toward bonds as the year nears: the glide path. Two funds with the same year can follow very different paths.

more risk, every dayLeveraged and inverse ETFs

A 3x fund tries to give three times the index's daily move; an inverse fund tries to go up when the index goes down. They reset every day, and that changes everything over weeks.

Before you buy any fund

Your broker app shows everything you need. Five checks, in order. Click through them.

1

Find the package

  • Mutual fund, ETF, or closed-end fund?
  • That tells you when you can trade and what price you'll get.

A closed-end fund can cost more or less than what it holds.

2

Index or active?

  • At the top of the description you'll find the goal: follow an index, or beat one.
  • If it's active, ask what you're paying extra for.

Most active large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 in a typical year.

3

Place it on the calm-to-nervous line

  • Money market, bonds, dividend stocks, a single sector, leveraged ETFs.
  • Match it to when you'll need the money.

Cash you need soon doesn't belong in a nervous fund.

4

Read the expense ratio

  • What it costs you every year, as a percent of your money.
  • Compare it with similar funds before you buy.

0.05% vs 1% was about $18,000 on $10,000 over 30 years.

5

Look at the top holdings

  • What's really inside, not just what the name says.
  • Especially for target-date and thematic funds.

Two funds with the same name or year can hold very different things.

Questions

What is the difference between an ETF and a mutual fund?

Mostly how you buy them. An ETF trades on the stock exchange like a single stock, any time the market is open. A mutual fund is bought from the fund company and priced once a day after the close, at its net asset value (NAV).

Is an index fund an ETF or a mutual fund?

It can be either. Index describes who decides what the fund buys: nobody, because it copies a list like the S&P 500. An S&P 500 index ETF and an S&P 500 index mutual fund own basically the same companies; only the way you buy them differs.

What is an expense ratio?

The yearly fee a fund charges, as a percentage of your money. Index funds often charge under 0.15%, stock mutual funds around 0.4% on average, and some active funds 1% or more.

How much do fund fees really cost?

In the video's example, $10,000 invested for 30 years at 7% a year before fees ends near $75,000 with a 0.05% fee and near $57,000 with a 1% fee: about $18,000 less, with nothing else changed.

Why do bond funds fall when interest rates rise?

If your old bond pays 3% and new bonds pay 5%, nobody pays full price for yours. Bond prices fall, and so does the fund. Funds of long-term bonds move the most.

Why do leveraged ETFs lose value in a flat market?

They reset every day. In the video's example, an index that goes up 3% and down 3% for 20 days ends less than 1% lower, but a 3x fund moving 9% each way ends almost 8% lower.

Three questions, then the fee.

The next time you hear about a fund you've never seen, you'll know what to ask: how you buy it, who decides what it buys, and what's inside. Then look at the one number that's charged every year no matter what. Learning how funds work is one thing; picking one for your own money is your call.

  • A fund is a shared basket: one buy, many investments.
  • Mutual fund, ETF or closed-end is only the package.
  • "Index" means nobody picks; it isn't a package.
  • Check what's inside, not just the name.
  • Small fees compound into big gaps.
  • Leveraged ETFs are built for short-term traders.
