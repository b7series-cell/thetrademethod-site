Get Better Faster

The fastest way to get good at trading isn't skipping the work. It's stopping the wrong work. Shorten the feedback loop: see the setup, trade it, review it, correct it, and see it again, until the pattern becomes obvious.

How do you get better at trading faster?

Shorten the feedback loop. Pick one setup, trade it, review it, correct it and see it again, until the pattern becomes obvious. Busy isn't the same as improving

Most traders hear “get good fast” and think shortcut: an indicator they haven't found, a setup they haven't learned, a trader or a Discord to follow. You can spend three years doing all of that and convince yourself you're learning.

Shorten the feedback loop

  • If none of it turns into better execution, cleaner risk, better review and a more repeatable process, you're not improving. You're staying busy.
  • The traders who improve fastest usually aren't the ones who know the most.
  • They see a setup, trade it, review it, correct it and see it again, and they repeat that cycle until the pattern becomes obvious.

That cycle is the real speed hack. Everything on this page is a way to run it more often, with cleaner data.

1 · Get good at one setup, not “trading”

Trading is too broad: thousands of stocks, options, futures, crypto, scalps, swings, momentum, mean reversion, news, tape. If the goal is “get good at trading”, you've already made it too hard.

Explain it before it happens

1

One situation, deep

  • Pick one setup that shows up often enough to study it, recognize it and improve at it.
  • Opening momentum through the premarket high, a 10 or 15-minute opening range break, VWAP continuation, a day three breakout, a liquidity trap, a mean reversion after a stock gets too extended.
  • The exact setup matters less than the depth.

Don't try to become good at everything. Pick one trade and become annoying about it.

2

An interest is not an edge

  • Most developing traders aren't bad because they have no ideas. They're bad because every trade is a new idea.
  • They see something moving, react to it, and afterward try to explain why it was a setup. That's backwards.
  • If you can't answer the six questions in this section before the trade, you don't have a setup yet. You have an interest in the stock.

The first way to speed up is to narrow the game.

2 · Build a real playbook

“I like breakouts” isn't a playbook. That's a sentence. A real playbook has examples, rules, variables and lots of screenshots: the best versions of the trade, the average versions, and the traps that looked close but failed.

Same chart, different opportunity

3

Rules get you started

  • Break every trade of your setup down the same way, with the fields on the right.
  • That's how you stop saying “this setup works” and start understanding when it works.
  • Most traders want a rule. Good traders want the nuance behind the rule.

The rules get you started. The nuance is where the money is.

4

A hundred examples of one strategy

  • A breakout with fresh news, high RVOL, room overhead and a strong market isn't the same as a breakout in a random name with no catalyst, resistance overhead and a so-so market.
  • On the surface both charts look like breakouts. They aren't the same opportunity.
  • Take screenshots, save charts, mark them up, put them in categories. You don't need 50 strategies; you need 100 examples of one.

That's what builds pattern recognition, and what gets newer traders to stop hesitating.

3 · Compress your reps

Experience doesn't just mean sitting in front of the screen every day. If you only count live trades, your setup shows up once a day, maybe once a week, and sometimes you miss it. That's too slow.

Same calendar, more reps

5

Every look is a rep

  • Live trading is one rep. A replay is another. Chart review is another. Historical study and backtesting is another.
  • Watching tape is another. Reviewing the best opportunities of the day, the week or the month is another.
  • Show yourself the same setup 500 times and you stop thinking about it the same way. You just start to see it.

Same calendar time, more useful reps. That's what getting good fast actually means.

6

Intuition is reps, not magic

  • You'll know when it's clean and when something is off: the stock isn't acting right, the volume isn't there, the entry is too early, the move is too extended, the market isn't helping.
  • People call that market intuition. It's pattern recognition built from reps.
  • The trader who only trades live and never reviews is moving slowly.

Trade live, screenshot it, review it after the close, study the best past examples, replay it. Same day, far more learning.

4 · Learn what actually moves stocks

Charts matter, levels matter, price action matters. But if you only look at the chart, you're missing a huge part of the trade. The chart tells you what is happening; the catalyst and positioning help you understand why the move might continue.

7

Why this stock, why today, why now

  • Who is trapped? Who needs to buy it? Who needs to sell it?
  • Is it fresh news, earnings, a sector move, a short squeeze, a macro headline, an analyst event, or a random move with no real reason behind it?
  • A day three breakout isn't just a chart pattern. It's a behavior pattern: positioning, trapped traders, momentum, and how people respond after a stock has proven it can move.

A liquidity trap isn't just a line on a chart. It's where people are leaning, where they're wrong, and what happens when price forces them out.

8

A chart becomes a case study

  • The fastest developing traders don't just save charts. They mark them up and save the context with them.
  • What the market was doing, what the sector was doing, what the news was, what the expectations were, what people were thinking coming into the day.
  • Build enough case studies and you stop asking “is this green candle good?”

You start asking “have I seen this situation before?” That's a completely different level of trading.

5 · Risk small enough to learn

The quickest way to slow down your development, or end it in a few months, is to trade too big. When the size is too big you stop learning and start surviving, and your data becomes useless.

9

Too big, and you study your emotions

  • You cut winners early because you're afraid to give money back, and hold losers because the loss feels too big to take.
  • You hesitate on good setups because you're still thinking about the last trade, and force trades to make money back.
  • You're not studying your strategy anymore. You're studying what you do when you're emotional.

In the learning phase, your job isn't to maximize P&L. It's to maximize quality reps.

10

Earn the right to size up

  • Paper trading forever, with no pressure at all, has its limits too. The money should mean something.
  • But the size has to be small enough that you can still make good decisions. Cut it down until you can follow the trade plan.
  • You don't size up because you want to make more money. You size up because your execution, your edge and your data justify it.

That's how professional traders think about size.

The too-big test

6 · Find the see-it moment

After a trade, especially a losing one, don't just ask whether the stop was in the right place. Ask: when should I have known? When was the moment the trade stopped acting right?

When should I have known?

11

Where the moment shows up

  • Sometimes before the entry: the stock never really confirmed, but you wanted it to, so you took it anyway.
  • Sometimes right after the entry: you got the trigger, but the follow-through just isn't there.
  • Sometimes halfway to the stop: price says the thesis is weakening, and you ignore it because technically the stop hasn't hit.

If your only risk tool is a full stop, you lose a full R every time you're wrong.

12

Reading behavior, not nerves

  • Not guessing, not panic selling, not bailing because you're nervous. Reading behavior against the version of the setup you've studied.
  • Get good at it and you stop taking unnecessary full losses, cutting risk earlier when the thesis weakens.
  • Just as important: you hold longer when the trade is acting exactly the way it should.

You'll rarely see it in real time at first. You see it after the close. After seeing it after the close 50 times, you start seeing it during the day.

7 · Review like an athlete watches film

Probably the least exciting part, and probably the most important. Not vague review (“I looked at my trades and I think I did okay”) but real review, with questions that have answers.

What the data says the problem is

13

Most traders are wrong about their problem

  • They think it's entries, and the data says they're taking low-quality trades.
  • They think it's stock selection, and the data says their best ideas work and their random trades are killing them.
  • They think it's discipline, and the data says they trade fine in the morning and fall apart after 11:30.

You don't know until you do this kind of review.

14

Review the winners too

  • Your winners, and the few things you do well at first, are where your edge lives.
  • Pull up 20 or 30 charts of your best trades side by side. What time of day? What was the market doing? What was the catalyst, the volume, what did the stock do before your entry?
  • How did you risk it, and where did it give you a chance to add?

Losers teach you what to avoid. Winners teach you what to look for and what to push. You need both.

The daily review

Write the answers down. Every day.

8 · Get around traders who are better than you

You could figure it all out alone. It just takes much longer and costs more. Not so they can tell you what to buy, which is the wrong way to use better traders. The value is in how they think.

15

You don't know what you don't know

  • You might spend three months fixing your entries when the real issue is that you're trading the wrong stocks.
  • You might think you need more confidence when the real issue is that your setup isn't defined.
  • You might think you need more screen time when the real issue is that you're not reviewing the screen time you already have.

One honest piece of feedback can save you months, sometimes years.

16

Your environment becomes your norm

  • Around traders who prepare seriously, review seriously and hold high standards, that becomes normal.
  • Around traders who chase alerts, complain, post wins and hide losses, and jump strategies every week, that becomes normal too.
  • Who you're around changes what you think trading is supposed to look like.

Choose carefully.

Feedback that saves months

The kind a better trader can give you in one look.

  • That wasn't actually an A setup.
  • You entered too early.
  • You sized that like an A trade, but it was really a B.
  • You missed the real opportunity because you were staring at the wrong ticker today.

9 · Stop looking for the fast way

The annoying truth: the search for the fast way is what makes traders slow.

Twenty restarts, or one build

17

Every restart resets the clock

  • Every week spent hunting for a new strategy is a week without reps on your current one.
  • Every hour watching random market opinions is an hour not reviewing your own trades.
  • Every time you scrap a setup after a normal losing stretch, you reset the clock.

That's how traders spend years in the market and are still beginners. They never built anything; they started over 20 times.

18

Then, and only then, add another

  • Once your execution on one setup is consistent and the data backs it, you've actually got something.
  • Then you can add a second playbook, and run the same process on it.
  • Not because it sounds exciting. Because it compounds.

The learning cycle

The whole page in one line: compress the learning cycle. See it, trade it, review it, correct it, see it again. Do that hundreds of times, then thousands of times.

1

See it

  • Recognize the setup you've studied: the stock in play, the level, the base forming under it.
  • Before the trigger, you can already answer the six questions: why it's in play, level, trigger, wrong, target, pass.
  • If it doesn't look like the version in your playbook, it isn't your trade today.

Seeing it is the skill the reps build. At first you'll only see it in review.

2

Trade it

  • Take it the way you wrote it: the trigger, the stop, the target.
  • Small enough that you could follow the plan, so the result is data on the setup, not on your nerves.
  • Watch how it acts after the entry. That's the information for the see-it moment.

You're maximizing quality reps, not P&L.

3

Review it

  • After the close, screenshot it and save the context: the catalyst, the market, the sector, the volume.
  • Best trade, worst trade, what you missed, what you forced, what showed up more than once.
  • File it with the best versions, the average ones, or the traps.

Review it like film. That's where the real problems show up.

4

Correct it

  • Find the one thing to fix: an entry before the close, a trade after 11:30, a B setup sized like an A.
  • Turn it into a rule in the playbook, so the next attempt is different.
  • Then ask when you should have known, and mark the see-it moment.

One mistake, one fix. Not a new strategy.

5

See it again

  • The next time the setup shows up, live, in replay or in your history, you see it a little faster and a little cleaner.
  • Rep 2, rep 50, rep 500. Each one tightens the feedback loop.
  • That's how you get good at trading as fast as humanly possible.

Not by skipping the work. By making sure the work you're doing actually builds skill.

Questions

Why one setup instead of trading in general?

Trading is too broad to get good at. One setup that shows up often enough to study, recognize and improve at gives you depth. The page's goal is 100 examples of one setup, not 50 strategies.

How do you know if you have a setup?

You can answer six questions before the trade: why the stock is in play, the level, the trigger, where you're wrong, the target, and what would make you pass.

How do you get more reps on a setup?

Add review to live trading: replays, chart review after the close, the best opportunities of the day, historical examples and backtests, and watching the tape. One live rep a day can become about nine.

Is market intuition real?

It's pattern recognition built from reps. Show yourself the same setup 500 times and you stop thinking about it and start seeing it.

See it, trade it, review it, correct it, see it again.

The fastest path isn't the exciting one. One setup, studied deeply, traded small, reviewed every time, with rules built from what you actually see. Same calendar time as everyone else. Far more useful reps.

  • Narrow the game to one setup you can explain before it happens.
  • Collect examples until the nuance is obvious.
  • Count every replay, review and study session as a rep.
  • Keep the size small enough that the data means something.
  • Find the see-it moment, and review the winners too.
