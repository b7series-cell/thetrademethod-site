Expected Move

Pick a ticker and an expiration, add the options prices, and get the range the options market is pricing, drawn on the chart. About 68% odds it finishes inside by expiration. Then see whether that range is bigger or smaller than the stock usually moves.

What is the expected move?

The expected move is the range the options market is pricing for a stock between now and an expiration, usually one standard deviation either side of the price. In a simplified bell-curve model, price has about a 68% chance of finishing inside it at expiration. This page's calculator draws it on a chart from the at-the-money straddle, a straddle plus strangle, implied volatility or the 16 delta strikes.

What the expected move is, and what 68% really means

The range the options market prices between now and an expiration, usually one standard deviation either side of the price. The options market sets it; you read it off the chain.

1

68% is where it finishes, not where it travels

  • In a simplified bell-curve model, about 68% of outcomes finish inside one standard deviation at expiration.
  • It says nothing about the path. Price can poke outside on Wednesday and still finish inside on Friday.
  • In the same model, the odds of never trading outside the range all week are only about 37%, and each edge gets touched roughly a third of the time.

So a touch of the top or bottom mid-week is normal. It isn't the range failing.

2

Any expiration you like

  • The weekly move uses the coming Friday's expiration. A common monthly read uses the standard monthly about a month out.
  • It works best where the options market is deep and liquid: SPY, QQQ, big names like AAPL. Not a thinly traded penny stock.
3

Take the reading while the market is closed

  • For the weekly move, read the chain after Friday's close and before Monday's open, when prices are settled.
  • The dashboard's Friday close setting does the same thing: the range is anchored to that close all week.

Why 68%

Four ways to read it off the chain

A straddle is the call and the put at the same strike. A strangle is a call and a put at two different strikes. Delta is roughly the market's odds an option finishes in the money.

4

The at-the-money straddle, × 1.25

  • Add the call and put at the strike nearest the price. SPY on June 3, 2022: the 411 call and 411 put, $9.91 on the ask.
  • The straddle isn't a full one-standard-deviation move. It's worth about 0.8 of one, because it prices the average size of the move, not its spread.
  • So a common approximation is expected move ≈ straddle × 1.25: $9.91 × 1.25 ≈ ±$12.39.

The straddle on its own covers roughly 58% odds. Handy, but narrower than the 68% range.

5

Straddle + first ITM strangle, ÷ 2

  • Add the first in-the-money call and put to the straddle, then halve the total. SPY: $9.91 + $10.47 = $20.38, ÷ 2 = $10.19.
  • In this example that matched the figure the platform showed. It's a rule of thumb that reproduces one platform's number, not a universal formula.
  • At $10.19 it sits closer to 0.8σ than a full 1σ, so its odds are nearer 59% than 68%.
6

The 16 delta strikes

  • Find the call and the put where delta is about 16. SPY: about $422.25 on top and $397.50 below.
  • That's about ±$12.4 on average, almost exactly the straddle × 1.25. The two methods agree on the 1σ range.
  • It also shows skew: $13.04 of room down and $11.71 up, because puts carry more implied volatility.
7

Implied volatility

  • Expected move ≈ price × implied volatility × √(calendar days to expiration ÷ 365).
  • SPY at $410.54 with 6 days to go and a $12.39 move implies about 24% annualized volatility.

The dashboard converts whichever method you fill in into an implied volatility, so you can line it up against realized volatility.

Why platforms disagree

Brokers calculate their expected move differently: some show the straddle-based figure, some a blend like method 5, some a 1σ number from implied volatility. If yours looks small next to straddle × 1.25, it's probably showing a straddle-style number. Check how it's defined before you treat it as the 68% range.

What to use it for

Context for the week, not a to-the-penny target, and not a signal on its own.

8

Draw it, then leave it

  • Two horizontal lines at the top and bottom, from Friday's close to expiration. Copy levels on the dashboard gives you both numbers.
  • Inside the range, the move is ordinary. Trade your levels and setups as usual.
9

A break isn't a reversal signal

  • Outside the range, a brand-new entry in the direction of the move is a chase: price has already gone further than the week was priced for.
  • That doesn't make it a fade. Strong trends, earnings and macro news can carry price well past the implied range and keep it there.
  • Wait for your own setup, such as a pullback to a level, rather than shorting the top or buying the bottom because a line got crossed.
10

Check the price of the range

  • Implied vs actual, above, tells you whether this week's range is wide or narrow for this stock.
  • Rich (well above its usual moves): premium is expensive, often ahead of an event. Cheap: moves past the range are likelier than 68% suggests.

Questions

What is the expected move?

The range the options market is pricing for a stock between now and an expiration, usually quoted as one standard deviation either side of the current price. In a simplified normal model, price finishes inside a one-standard-deviation range about 68% of the time.

Does 68% mean price stays inside the range all week?

No. The 68% is roughly the chance price finishes inside the range at expiration. The chance it never trades outside the range before then is much lower, about 37% in the same model, because price can poke out and come back.

How do I calculate it from the at-the-money straddle?

Add the at-the-money call and put prices. The straddle is worth about 0.8 of a one-standard-deviation move, so a common approximation is expected move ≈ straddle × 1.25. The straddle on its own covers roughly 58% odds.

Is averaging the straddle and the ITM strangle the official formula?

No. Straddle plus the first in-the-money strangle, divided by two, reproduced one platform's figure in the SPY example, but it's a rule of thumb, not a universal formula. Different platforms calculate their expected move differently.

Is a break outside the expected move a reversal signal?

No. Outside the range a new entry in the direction of the move is a chase, but strong trends, earnings and macroeconomic news can carry price well beyond the implied range. Treat it as context and wait for your own setup.

How do I know whether options are pricing a big or small move?

Compare the implied move with the stock's actual moves over windows of the same length, which is what Implied vs actual does. If the implied move is bigger than about 68% of past moves, options are pricing more movement than usual; if it's smaller, less.

Is the data on this page live?

Prices come from a delayed market data feed, with the time and the delay shown under the chart. The options prices are the ones you type in, unless the site's data plan includes the options chain, in which case the dashboard fills them and says so. When live data isn't available the page switches to made-up example bars and labels them.
