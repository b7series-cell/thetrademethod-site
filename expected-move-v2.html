What the expected move is

The range where you'd expect price to be contained, with about 68% odds, over a given time period. The options market sets it. You just read it off the chain.

Why 68%

1

A range with about 68% odds

  • On a normal bell curve, about 34% of outcomes sit within one standard deviation above the middle and 34% within one below.
  • Together that's about 68%. The expected move is that one-standard-deviation band, in dollars.
  • Under normal market conditions, roughly two weeks out of three should finish inside it.

Which also means about one week in three finishes outside it. Real returns have fatter tails than the bell curve, so big misses happen more often than the textbook says.

2

Any expiration you like

  • As long as there's an options expiration, you can read the expected move to it.
  • The weekly move uses the expiration this coming Friday. A common monthly read uses the expiration about 31 days out.
  • It works best on assets with a very efficient, liquid options market: SPY, QQQ, big names like AAPL. Not an obscure penny stock where nobody trades the options.
3

Take the reading while the market is closed

  • For the weekly move, read it after Friday's close and before Monday's open.
  • The chain is calm and the prices are settled. Not while it's going off the wall during the session.

Do it once, draw it on the chart, and leave it there for the week.

You don't need a special platform

Thinkorswim and tastytrade print an expected move in the corner of the chain, and each platform calculates it its own way. Anywhere else, all you need is the chain and one of the methods below, or the calculator.

Methods 1 and 2 · price the straddle, then the strangle

A straddle is just the call and the put at the same strike. A strangle is a call and a put at two different strikes.

Reading it off the chain

4

Method 1 · the at-the-money straddle

  • Find the last traded price, Friday's close. SPY: $410.54.
  • Find the strike closest to it. $410.54 is above $410.50, so round up to 411.
  • Add the price of the 411 call and the 411 put, both on the ask: $9.91.

A very quick estimate. In all three examples it landed roughly 30 cents short of the platform's number.

5

Method 2 · add the first in-the-money strangle, divide by 2

  • Keep the straddle. Now go one strike in the money on each side so you're strangling the last price: the 410 call and the 411 put, again on the ask.
  • Straddle plus strangle: $20.38. That's way more than $10.19, but you just added two prices together.
  • Average them: $20.38 ÷ 2 = $10.19. The same number the platform showed.

One extra step for a much closer read. On QQQ it came out one penny off.

Ask or mid?

The video prices every leg on the ask, which is the conservative, slightly wide read. Most fills on a liquid chain happen near the mid (halfway between bid and ask). On SPY the spread is a penny or two, so it barely matters; on a wide chain the ask can overstate the move by several percent. The calculator shows both.

Method 3 · the 16 delta strikes

The most interesting of the three, because it brings implied volatility skew into play. Markets tend to fall faster than they rise, so the put side of the chain prices more movement than the call side.

Find 16 on both sides

6

Find the strike where delta is 16

  • Bring up the delta column on both sides of the chain.
  • Call side: SPY's 16 delta sits between the 422 and 422.50 strikes. Call it $422.25, the upper limit.
  • Put side: ignore the minus sign. 16 delta sits between 397 and 398. Call it $397.50, the lower limit.

Overall a slightly wider range than the other two methods, and a lopsided one.

7

Why 16 lines up with the bell curve

  • 16% is the tail beyond one standard deviation on a bell curve: 13.5% + 2.35% + the sliver past three.
  • Traders use delta as a shortcut for the odds of finishing in the money, so a 16 delta strike sits near the edge of the 1σ range.
  • The probability of touching the strike before expiration runs roughly twice that, about 31 to 35% on the chain, close to the 34% between the middle and the edge of the range.

The shortcut is close for short-dated, low-volatility chains like SPY's weekly. It drifts the longer and wilder the option gets; the table on the right shows how far.

Delta isn't exactly the odds

In Black-Scholes a call's delta is N(d1), but its odds of finishing in the money are N(d2), and d2 = d1 − σ√T. So a call's delta overstates its odds and a put's delta understates them, and the gap grows with volatility and time.

Model odds with no interest rate or dividends. They're risk-neutral, priced by the market, not a forecast of what will happen.

The math underneath · the IV formula and the Rule of 16

The chain methods read the expected move straight off option prices. Experienced traders also carry the textbook version in their head, built from implied volatility.

8

One standard deviation from implied volatility

Expected move ≈ S × IV × √DTE ÷ 365 S = price · IV = annual implied volatility as a decimal · DTE = calendar days to expiration

  • Implied volatility is quoted per year. The square root scales it down to the days you care about.
  • Example: $410.54 × 0.20 × √(6 ÷ 365) = about ±$10.53 for six days at 20% IV.
  • Some traders use trading days over 252 instead. Either works if you're consistent.

An at-the-money straddle is worth about 0.8 of this number in theory (√(2/π) ≈ 0.80), because a straddle pays the average move, not the one-standard-deviation move. That's one reason platforms, the straddle and the formula never agree to the penny.

9

The Rule of 16

  • There are about 252 trading days a year, and √252 ≈ 15.9. Round it to 16.
  • So implied volatility ÷ 16 is roughly the one-day expected move, in percent.
  • VIX at 16 means the market is pricing about 1% daily moves in the S&P 500. VIX at 32, about 2%.

A quick sanity check: if a stock's IV is 48%, a 3% day is an ordinary day for it, not news.

When the number gets distorted

The expected move is only as good as the option prices behind it. Three things bend it.

before the event

Events inflate it

Earnings, CPI, FOMC and Fed rate decisions are binary events. Option sellers charge more for the expiration that contains one, so implied volatility and the expected move jump for that week.

If earnings land inside your week, the weekly move includes the earnings move. Compare it with an expiration before the event to see how much of it is the event.

after the event

Then IV gets crushed

Once the number is out, the uncertainty is gone and implied volatility usually collapses the same day. The next expected move you read can be much narrower.

Stocks often settle into a tighter range after the announcement, but not always. A real surprise can keep running well past the old range.

pricing

Ask overstates, mid is fairer

Pricing on the ask adds half the bid-ask spread to every leg. On SPY that's pennies. On a thin chain with 20 or 30 cent spreads it can add several percent to the move.

If the spread is wide, use the mid, or skip that chain: a wide spread usually means the expected move isn't reliable there anyway.

What to use it for

Not a to-the-penny calculation. A piece of context for your trading week that helps you make better decisions.

Outside the move, you're chasing

10

Draw it, then leave it

  • Draw two horizontal lines at the top and bottom of the expected move. No indicator does this for you; it's two manual price levels.
  • Shade a rectangle between them if you like. That box is where price should spend about 68% of its weeks.
  • Color them the same every week so the box reads at a glance.
11

Cracking outside the range

  • Looking for new-money longs once price cracks outside the expected move to the upside? You're starting to chase.
  • Instead, think about the opposite side: is there reason to believe there's a retracement back lower, and a pullback you can participate in?
  • The same logic flips for new shorts below the bottom of the range.

Prices can and do move outside the range. It's not a perfect container for the week, just the added context that says chasing out here might not be wise.

Inside or outside?

One quick check before a new entry.

inside the rangeAn ordinary move

Trade your levels and setups as usual. Nothing unusual has been priced in yet.

outside the rangeDon't chase

The week's priced move is already done. Look for the pullback, or wait.

The methods at a glance

Each one gets you close to the platform's number. Pick the one your chain makes easiest.

The at-the-money straddle

  1. Take the last price.
  2. Find the strike nearest it.
  3. Add that strike's call and put.
SPY
411 call + 411 put$9.91

Fastest. Usually about 30 cents light of the platform.

Straddle + first ITM strangle, ÷ 2

  1. Price the straddle as in method 1.
  2. Add the first in-the-money call and put.
  3. Divide the total by 2.
SPY
$9.91 + $10.47$20.38
÷ 2±$10.19

Matched the platform within a penny or two in all three examples.

The 16 delta strikes

  1. Show the delta column on both sides.
  2. Find the call strike at about 16 delta: the top.
  3. Find the put strike at about −16 delta: the bottom.
SPY
Range$397.50 – $422.25

A little wider, and the only method that shows put skew.

The implied volatility formula

  1. Read the at-the-money IV for the expiration.
  2. Multiply price × IV × √(days ÷ 365).
  3. For one day, IV ÷ 16 gives the percent move.
example
$410.54 × 0.20 × √(6/365)±$10.53

The textbook cross-check. Needs IV, which most chains show.

Expected move · quick reference

What it is

One standard deviation of price to an expiration. About 68% odds price finishes inside; about 95% inside two.

When to read it

Weekly: after Friday's close, before Monday's open, on the coming Friday's expiration. Liquid chains only.

1 · ATM straddle

ATM call + ATM put
Fast, usually a little light.

2 · Straddle + ITM strangle

(straddle + 1st ITM call + 1st ITM put) ÷ 2
Closest to the platform's number.

3 · 16 delta strikes

+16Δ call strike / −16Δ put strike
Shows put skew; a little wider.

IV formula

S × IV × √(DTE ÷ 365)
Straddle ≈ 0.8 × this, in theory.

Rule of 16

IV ÷ 16 = one-day move %
VIX 16 ≈ 1% a day. VIX 32 ≈ 2%.

Watch out
  • Events inflate it; IV crush shrinks it after.
  • Ask pricing overstates on wide chains; use the mid.
  • Delta ≠ exact odds on long, high-IV options.
Using it
  • Draw the top and bottom; leave them all week.
  • Inside: ordinary move, trade as usual.
  • Outside: don't chase, look for the pullback.

It's not a target. It's how far the market thinks the week can go.

The options market prices a range every week, and about two weeks in three price stays inside it. Read it once after Friday's close, draw it, and let it tell you when a move is ordinary and when a new entry would be a chase.

  • Expected move = one standard deviation, about 68% odds.
  • Quick read: the at-the-money straddle.
  • Closer read: straddle plus first ITM strangle, divided by 2.
  • Skew read: the 16 delta call and put strikes.
  • Cross-check: price × IV × √(days ÷ 365).
  • Outside the range, think pullback, not chase.
