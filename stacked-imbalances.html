Stacked Imbalances

What is a stacked imbalance, and why do order flow traders key off it? It's three or more prices in a row where one side traded about four times harder than the other. Markets have memory, so when price comes back to that zone it's a place to get long or short with a small stop. Mark it. Wait for the return. See if it holds.

What an imbalance is

An imbalance is more aggressive buying than selling, or the other way around, measured on the volume that actually traded in the two-way auction. You can only see it on a footprint chart, which lists the contracts that traded on the bid and on the ask at every price inside a bar.

20 on the bid, 150 on the offer

1

Diagonal, not side by side

  • There's always a bid at one price and an offer at the next price up. Say the market is 5 bid at 6.
  • 20 lots trade on the bid at 5 and 150 trade on the offer at 6. Only 20 against 150: a buying imbalance.
  • A selling imbalance is the mirror: the bid at a price trading far more than the offer one tick above it.

Most footprint software compares this way, across the one-tick gap. Some traders compare the two numbers on the same row instead, like our Order Flow for Beginners page and the Futures Flow video below. Both are looking for the same thing: one side hitting far harder than the other.

How lopsided is lopsided?

There's no law. Pick a ratio and stick with it so your levels mean the same thing every day.

about 4 to 1The closest thing to a standard

Most people use something near 4 to 1, and it's what every walkthrough on this page uses.

3, 5 or 6 to 1Also in use

A lower ratio marks more imbalances and more noise. A higher one marks fewer, stronger ones.

Diagonal or horizontal?

Two ways to read the same footprint. Pick one and stay with it.

diagonal · orderflowsBid vs the offer one tick up

150 on the offer against 20 on the bid one tick lower. The way most footprint software sets it.

horizontal · futures flowBid vs ask at the same price

41 against 7 at one price: 400% or more is an imbalance. He reads NQ 5-minute bars on a 2-tick aggregation, so two stacked rows there are four ticks.

When they stack

One imbalance in a bar is worth noticing. When you start seeing more than one, it's time to pay attention. Three or more stacked neatly on top of each other, a buying imbalance at 5, at 6 and at 7, is a stacked imbalance.

One is noise, three is a level

2

Why a stack is a level

  • A stack means one side was aggressive at every price through a gap, with nobody on the other side able to slow it down.
  • Markets have memory. Liquidity has memory. When the market comes back to those prices, it often reacts.
  • So note where it happened. Most order flow platforms draw it forward as a line across the chart for you.

A buying stack is a place to look for longs on a return. A selling stack is a place to look for shorts.

Generated by what traded

These levels don't come from a formula. They come from the market itself: at these prices, aggressive buying beat aggressive selling, by a lot. That's why they're worth keying around.

buying stackAggressive buyers

The offers lifted, price after price. On a return, a level to look for longs.

selling stackAggressive sellers

The bids hit, price after price. On a return, a level to look for shorts.

Where it sits in the candle

Futures Flow's point, and the part most people miss: the same stack can mean the opposite thing depending on where it sits in the candle and which way price went first. A stack in the middle of the body is the side that won. A stack in a wick, with the first move going the other way, is passive orders and trapped traders.

Same stack, opposite meaning

3

Passive orders fill the other way

  • A limit sell gets filled when a buyer lifts the ask. So a column of buy imbalances can really be sellers resting their orders: passive sellers.
  • A limit buy gets filled when a seller hits the bid. A column of sell imbalances can be buyers soaking it up: passive buyers.
  • The tell is the reaction. Buy imbalances at the top of a red candle and then a move down? Those buyers are trapped.

Trapped-trader stacks in the wicks are the ones he trades most. Mid-body stacks are the ones he marks for support and resistance later.

What makes a stack worth marking

His filters, before a stack goes on the chart as a level.

keepMiddle of a strong body

A big candle with little wick at the close, two or more stacked levels in the middle of the body, and a first reaction away from it.

skip or be carefulRight at the close, or alone

A single imbalance, a stack right near the close or in the lower third, a stack at the low of day, or one where price went straight through first.

Markets have memory: the payroll stack

On a normal bar or candle chart you see the open, the high, the low and the close. You can't see why a market pulls back to a price and stops there. Usually something happened in the order flow at that price earlier. Here's the example from the video, on non-farm payroll Friday.

Bid 3,974, stop 3,973

4

Market-generated information

  • The payroll bar ripped straight up. A lot of aggressive buying, not much liquidity in the way, and a stacked buying imbalance left right in the middle of the bar, around 3,973 to 3,974.
  • The market went up, came off, and came right back down to 3,974.
  • If you knew that level, you just put a bid in around 3,974 with a stop at 3,973. It held one bar, two bars, three bars, four bars, then rallied to 3,994.

His own words on it: a trade that comes right to the level, holds it perfectly and rallies 20 points is not normal. Don't take the best example as the usual one. Take it as proof the level is real.

Not every stack holds

He's blunt about it: this isn't a trade you mortgage your house on. Four returns to a stack from the video, side by side, so you know what each one looks like when it happens to you.

Same signal, four outcomes

5

Is the market paying me?

  • Some stacks hold and pay quickly. A little three-bar stack bought around 86 rallied into the 90s: a quick five points.
  • Some overshoot by a tick before they go. Price came back, went one tick past the level, then ran about 20 points.
  • Some just go sideways. If the market isn't paying you, get out and wait for something with more push behind it.
  • Some fail outright. The aggressive sellers who were there earlier aren't interested anymore, and price takes the level out.

That's why the stop sits just past the stack. When the level is wrong you find out fast, and it costs a point, not a day.

Something to build a strategy around

Stacked imbalances don't replace a plan. They give you a level made by real trading, so the plan has somewhere precise to enter and somewhere precise to be wrong.

The short side: check the sellers are still there

Same idea, upside down. A selling stack came in around 3,921 to 3,922. If the market comes back up there, doesn't push too far into it, and aggressive selling is still coming in, that's a short.

Barely in, sellers still there

6

Two things to read at the return

  • Depth. A level that's holding doesn't get penetrated much. A tick or two in is fine. Deep inside it, the level is in doubt.
  • Who's aggressive now. At a selling stack you want to see aggressive selling still coming in when price gets there. At a buying stack, buyers.

The stack tells you where to look. The order flow at the return tells you whether to act.

The trigger: wait for a large participant

Futures Flow doesn't just leave a limit order at the stack. He waits for the return to show him someone big defending it, usually on a smaller chart like a range chart, and only then takes the initial reaction.

No participant, no trade

7

Spotting a large participant

  • Watch the prints at the level. If it's trading 10 lots, 20 lots, 30 lots, and then 128 shows up within half a second, that's probably a large buyer.
  • Check the candle's duration too. A candle that fills that size and closes green in about 30 seconds tells you someone wanted in.
  • Heavy negative delta into a buying stack is a good sign, not a bad one: those sellers are the ones who can get trapped.

On a 5-minute chart you'd be 15 points late waiting for the close. That's why he drops to a smaller chart for the entry.

Support flips to resistance

A stack that was supposed to be resistance and broke can turn into support, and the other way around, just like an old level on an hourly chart. Keep the marked zone after it breaks and watch how price treats it from the other side.

The NQ reactions he showed off stacks on his chart: about 26, 33, 43, 50 and 55 points.

Rules of thumb

Short ones, from both videos. Orderflows says it himself: it's not brain surgery. People over-complicate the market. Trade what's right in front of you.

session

US hours first

He prefers stacks made during the US session. The overnight session trades less volume, so its imbalances carry less weight.

chart

Footprint only

A bar or candle chart can't show an imbalance. You need a footprint chart to see where the stacks are.

timing

Freshest is best

Order flow is best used as soon as it appears. When the number hits and a stack prints, you have a level for the rest of the morning.

exit

Sideways is a signal

If the trade is going sideways on you, get out and wait for the next one. Doesn't work? Fine. Wait for the next trade.

location

Body, not the close

Mark stacks in the middle of a strong body. Near the close, in the lower third, or at the low of day, be careful.

reaction

First move away

The best stacks get an initial reaction away, then a return. If price went straight through first, it's a weaker, riskier level.

Trading a stacked imbalance, step by step

The whole idea in five steps. Click through them.

1

Spot the imbalances

  • On a footprint chart, compare each offer with the bid one tick below it, and each bid with the offer one tick above it.
  • About 4 to 1 or more is an imbalance. Keep the same ratio every day.

A buying imbalance is aggressive buyers. A selling imbalance is aggressive sellers.

2

Mark three or more in a row

  • One alone means little. Three or more stacked at consecutive prices is a stacked imbalance.
  • Mark the zone and carry it forward. Favor stacks in the middle of a strong body, from the US session, with a first move away.

It's a level the market made, not one a formula made.

3

Wait for the return

  • Don't chase the bar that made the stack. Let price leave and come back.
  • Markets and liquidity have memory: the return is where the trade is.

No return, no trade. There's always another stack.

4

Read the return

  • Is it holding, without pushing far into the stack?
  • Is the same side still aggressive there: buyers at a buying stack, sellers at a selling stack? Better still, does a large participant show up?

If it blows straight through, the traders who made the stack aren't there anymore.

5

Trade it with a small stop

  • Enter at the stack, stop at or just past its far end. On the payroll trade: bid 3,974, stop 3,973.
  • If it pays, let it. If it goes sideways, get out. If it fails, take the small loss.

Small risk is what makes the 10 to 1 and 20 to 1 winners possible, and the losers cheap.
