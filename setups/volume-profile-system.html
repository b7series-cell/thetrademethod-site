Watch the system work one session

1

Read the profile, not just the price

  • A traditional volume indicator measures volume across time — one bar per period. The volume profile measures the same volume across price instead, showing exactly which levels attracted the trading activity.
  • The point of control (PC) marks the single price with the highest volume in the range — the longest bar. The value area high and low (VAH/VAL) bound the zone holding 70% of all volume traded, the levels where most of the activity actually happened.

Long bars are where both sides were actively trading — price tends to slow down and consolidate there. Short bars are where one side dominated — price tends to move through them quickly. That rhythm is what the three setups below are built on.

2

Place it on the previous session — never the current one

  • Draw the fixed-range volume profile across the full previous session, open to close, then use its PC and value area as reference levels for the session that's trading right now.
  • Tool settings: row size 60, value area volume 70%, with VH, VAL, and PC all enabled.

The current session's profile is still being built candle by candle, so its levels keep shifting in real time — trading off them is trading off a moving target. Yesterday's profile is finished and stable.

3

Setup 1 — the PC bounce

  • Valid only when the previous session closed outside its value area. Mark the PC and wait for the current session's price to return to it.
  • Confirm with a reversal candle at the level — a rejection wick through the PC that closes back below it, an engulfing candle, or a run of candles in the new direction — before entering.

The PC is the level the market agreed on most. A touch alone isn't an entry — the previous-session condition and the confirming candle are what separate a real reaction from a random pass through the level.

4

Setup 2 — the value area reversal

  • Valid only when the previous session closed inside its value area. Wait for the current session's price to cross outside the value area high or low, then wait for a full candle to close back inside it — a wick alone doesn't count.
  • Stop goes near the swing high or low the move crossed outside from; target is fixed at 2R.

Moving outside the value area means price is trading at a relative premium or discount to where most of the volume traded — a close back inside is the market rejecting that extension, not just touching it.

5

Setup 3 — the value area breakout

  • Works regardless of how the previous session closed. Requires price to break significantly outside the value area, then pull back and hold near it — it doesn't have to hold the level perfectly, just not push too deep back inside.
  • Final trigger is a break of structure in the breakout direction: a higher high for a long setup, a lower low for a short.

A breakout that holds its pullback and then prints a fresh structure break is showing real continuation, not just a one-candle spike that's about to get reclaimed.

6

One system, not three separate strategies

  • All three read off the same profile and the same previous-session condition decides which one is even in play — closed outside the value area points to the PC bounce, closed inside points to the reversal, and a clean breakout can override either one as the session develops.
  • Every setup is sized the same way: a defined stop specific to that setup, and a fixed 2R target.

A reversal that stops out doesn't mean the tool failed — the same session's price action can hand you a breakout setup right after, which is why these are read together as one system, not three competing ideas.

Previous session — use this

Fully closed, open to close. Its PC and value area are finished and stable, so they hold as reference levels for the session trading right now.

Current session — never this

Still forming candle by candle. Its profile keeps changing in real time, which makes any level drawn from it unreliable and prone to misleading signals.

The four filters

The tool draws the same levels either way — these are what keep you from trading a level the setup's own rules haven't actually qualified yet.

1  Profiling the current session

Drawing the volume profile across today's still-forming session instead of yesterday's completed one, then trading off levels that are still shifting in real time.

Kills: reference levels that move under you while you're trying to trade them.

2  A PC bounce with the wrong prior close

Taking a PC touch as an entry signal without first confirming the previous session actually closed outside its value area.

Kills: treating every touch of the point of control as the same setup, when only one condition makes it valid.

3  A wick instead of a close

Counting a wick back into the value area as reversal confirmation instead of waiting for a full candle to actually close back inside it.

Kills: entering on a level that price only touched in passing, not one it actually accepted.

4  A pullback that doesn't hold

Taking the breakout setup when the pullback pushes too deep back into the value area, or never holds near the level at all.

Kills: a "breakout" that was really just a spike about to get fully reclaimed.

The mistake this prevents

Sizing the stop off a round number or a comfort level instead of the level the setup actually defines — just beyond the PC, the swing high or low, or the break-of-structure candle.

A stop that isn't anchored to the setup's own invalidation point makes the fixed 2R target meaningless — the ratio only holds if the risk side is measured correctly in the first place.

Define the stop from the setup's own structure first. The 2R target follows from that, never the other way around.
