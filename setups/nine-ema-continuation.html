What is a 9 EMA continuation trade?

A 9 EMA continuation is a pullback entry after a breakout on real volume. Instead of chasing the spike, you wait for price to come back to the 9-period EMA, watch whether buyers step back in, and enter when price reclaims the line with strength, with a stop below the pullback low.

Catalyst & the miss rate

Watch one 9 EMA continuation

1

Wait for the pullback — don't chase the move

  • When a stock breaks out of a range on real volume, chasing it puts you in a defensive posture before you've even gotten in.
  • Instead, wait for the opportunity to be aggressive: let price come back and test the 9 EMA rather than buying the initial spike.

Buying the breakout itself means you have no idea where you're wrong. Waiting for the pullback gives you an actual level to define your risk against.

2

Read the tape at the line, not just the touch

  • Don't blindly buy the first touch of the 9 EMA. Watch the interplay between price action and the bid as price comes into the line — are buyers stepping up, or are sellers hitting every lift?
  • A dip below the 9 EMA with no clean buying response is not a reason to give up on the trade. It's a reason to focus even harder on the bid for the moment buyers actually show up.

The line itself doesn't trigger the trade. What the tape is doing around the line is the actual signal — the 9 EMA is just where you're watching it happen.

3

Enter on the reclaim, not the tap

  • The trigger is buyers visibly stepping back in and price reclaiming the 9 EMA with strength — not simply price touching the line.
  • A more mechanical version of the same trade: buy the first candle that closes back above the 9 EMA after the first touch, without needing a clean break-and-reclaim first. In that version a break below the 9 EMA ends the idea. Both versions work: the reclaim version gets a better risk/reward, the touch version wins more often.

Two traders can take the exact same setup differently and both be right. What matters is that you know which version you're taking and you're consistent about it.

4

Stop below the pullback low

  • Risk is defined at the low of the candle, or the low of the pullback attempt, that tested the 9 EMA — not an arbitrary distance from your entry.
  • This is usually a small, well-defined risk relative to the size of the move you're positioning for, which is what makes the trade worth taking in the first place.

A small, defined stop against a level the price action itself gave you is what lets you size the trade intelligently instead of guessing.

5

Manage it with a three-exit plan

  • Worst case: price stuffs and takes out the low of your entry candle. You're stopped out, small loss, move on.
  • Take roughly half off on an acceleration after an acceleration — a clear parabolic push or blow-off move away from the 9 EMA.
  • Trail the remainder with a close below the 9 EMA as a full trailing stop — often reading your entry on a faster chart (2-minute) and managing the trail one step slower (5-minute).
  • The second trader's version: don't sell anything for the first 30 minutes, since the move often runs 35 minutes to an hour. Trail on a two-bar break or a 5-minute close under the 9 EMA, and get out early only on a parabolic, high-volume push into a whole number.

Selling everything into the first sign of strength gives back the best part of the trade. A defined three-step plan lets you actually capture what the setup is offering instead of guessing in real time.

6

Give it a real catalyst, and expect the miss rate

  • This works best with a real reason behind the move — a catalyst like earnings, breaking pre-market or prior-day highs, a 5-day range breakout, or ideally a 52-week breakout. The second trader wants the pre-market high broken first, then the pullback to the 9 EMA; it often lines up with the pre-market high or VWAP.
  • Roughly two times out of ten, the stock simply never comes back to give you the touch at all. That's a normal, acceptable miss rate — not a reason to chase the ones that run without you.

The amount of energy traders spend worrying about the setups that just run away is disproportionate to how often it actually happens. Let those go and wait for the next one.

Which touch you can trust

Two ways to time the same trade

Different traders, different time frames, same underlying read.

Read the tape around the reclaim

More discretionary, on the 1-minute chart: let price dip under the line, watch the bid, and buy when buyers step back in and it reclaims the 9 EMA.

Trade the mechanical touch-and-confirm

Buy the first candle that closes back above the 9 EMA after the first touch, on a 2 or 5-minute chart, with a tight, defined stop. A break below the 9 EMA ends the idea.

Trail one time frame slower

If you read your entry on a faster chart, manage the trailing stop one step slower — a 2-minute entry, trailed on the 5-minute.

The four filters

These don't find the trade — the pullback and the reclaim do that. They keep you off the versions that only look like it.

1  No real catalyst behind the move

Earnings, a gap, breaking pre-market or prior-day highs, ideally a 52-week breakout — something has to be driving it.

Kills: trading the touch on a stock with nothing behind it.

2  Sellers keep hitting the bid

If every lift off the 9 EMA gets sold into, that's not a reclaim yet — it's still information, not a trigger.

Kills: buying a reclaim that hasn't actually happened.

3  Chasing the initial breakout

Buying the spike instead of waiting for the pullback puts you in a defensive posture from the first tick.

Kills: entering with no real level to define your risk against.

4  No exit plan before you're in

Deciding when to take profit and when to trail has to happen before the trade starts working, not during it.

Kills: giving back the move because there was no plan for it.

The mistake this prevents

Buy the very first touch of the 9 EMA on impulse — say, right at 64.50, before any reclaim has actually happened — with no confirmation and often no stop decided yet.

If sellers keep hitting the bid after that, you're now in a trade the tape never actually confirmed, guessing at a stop after the fact.

The touch tells you where to watch. The reclaim, with buyers actually stepping in, is what tells you to act.

Why the 9 EMA specifically

Not every moving average behaves the same way, and the difference is the whole reason this one gets used here.

A simple moving average weights every day in its lookback equally — a 200-day SMA treats a print from about ten months ago exactly the same as yesterday's, which makes it slow by design: useful for anchoring a long-term view, but it's already well behind by the time it turns. An exponential moving average weights recent price much more heavily and lets older data fade out geometrically, which gives it something closer to a memory span than a flat lookback window — a 200-period EMA behaves like it's really tracking the last couple of months, not the full 200 days equally. Compress that same idea down to a 9-period EMA on an intraday chart, and what you get is a line that tracks the order flow of traders who have to react right now — not a lagging read on what already happened, but a live pulse of whether the buyers pushing this move are still actually there.

    alerts

    A breakout is a close over the high of the last 12 bars, above the 9 EMA, on at least 1.5× the 20-bar average volume. It isn't bought. The first later bar whose low reaches the 9 EMA (within 12 bars) is the touch, and each close back under the line after that is marked as a failed test. The first green candle that closes back over the 9 EMA is the entry, with the stop one tick under the pullback low and share size worked out from the risk you type in. //@version=6
// =====================================================================
// 9 EMA Continuation — thetrademethod.com
//
// Don't chase the breakout. Wait for the pullback into the 9 EMA, then buy
// the reclaim, not the touch. For intraday charts (5-minute, like the
// page's walkthrough and example chart). Long only by default, as the page
// shows it; shorts are an optional mirror.
//
//   1. Breakout   A bar CLOSES above the highest high of the 12 bars before
//                 it, above the 9 EMA, on volume at least 1.5x the average
//                 of the 20 bars before it. No entry on that bar.
//   2. Touch      The first later bar whose LOW reaches the 9 EMA, within 12
//                 bars of the breakout. No touch in time = "no touch" (the
//                 move ran without you), setup dropped.
//   3. Failed     After the touch, every close under the 9 EMA is a failed
//      tests      test. Not a reason to quit.
//   4. Reclaim    The first GREEN candle after the touch that closes back
//                 above the 9 EMA. Buy that close. Stop one tick under the
//                 pullback low (lowest low from the touch to the reclaim).
//   5. Void       A close under the breakout bar's low, or the session's
//                 last bar, ends the setup.
//   6. Exits      Half off at +2R (our fixed stand-in for the video's
//                 "acceleration after an acceleration"). The rest comes out
//                 on the first CLOSE under the 9 EMA, or at the session's
//                 last bar. Stop first, then the half target, then the
//                 close. One trade at a time.
//
// Gap days: the 9 EMA starts from yesterday's close, so after a big gap
// it needs ten or more bars to catch up. On a 5- or 15-minute chart it can
// still be far below price when the first pullback comes, and the setup is
// marked "no touch". The video's traders read gap days on the 1- and
// 2-minute charts, where the line catches up within minutes.
//
// Same rules as the page's example chart (js/ema9-live.js). The 12-bar
// range, 1.5x volume, 12-bar wait and the 2R half are ours, not the
// video's. Not coded: the catalyst (news, earnings, a 52-week break), so
// check that yourself. Signals use closed bars only. Draws and alerts;
// it places no orders.
// =====================================================================

Questions

Do you buy the first touch of the 9 EMA?

No. The touch only tells you where to watch. The trigger is buyers stepping back in and price reclaiming the 9 EMA. A mechanical version buys the first candle that closes back above the 9 EMA after the first touch.

Where is the stop on a 9 EMA pullback?

Below the low of the candle, or of the pullback, that tested the 9 EMA, not an arbitrary distance from the entry.

What if the stock never pulls back to the 9 EMA?

About two times in ten it never gives you the touch. That's a normal miss rate, not a reason to chase. Let it go and wait for the next one.

Does the 9 EMA continuation work?

Our test with the page's indicator rules lost a little: 7,094 trades on 5-minute bars across 10 large-cap stocks, Nov 2021 to Nov 2026, with a 33% win rate, a 0.93 profit factor and -0.03R per trade. Three of the 10 stocks were positive.

Read the reclaim. Not the touch.

The 9 EMA isn't a magic line — it's just where you watch the tug-of-war between buyers and sellers play out. The reclaim is the signal. The line is only where you're standing to see it.

  • Don't chase the breakout. Wait for the pullback and let the 9 EMA give you a level to actually define risk against.
  • Sellers hitting the bid on the first test isn't a reason to abandon the idea — it's a reason to watch even closer for the real reclaim.
  • About two times out of ten, it never comes back at all. That's a normal cost of the setup, not a signal to chase the ones that run without you.
