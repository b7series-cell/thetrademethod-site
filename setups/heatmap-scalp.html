What is the Heatmap Scalp?

The Heatmap Scalp is an order flow day trade on NQ and ES futures. Higher lows above VWAP means longs only, and shorts are the mirror. On a pullback, a liquidity heatmap such as Bookmap shows when the other side's big orders stop moving price and the bid holds. Fixed dollar stop, add on pullbacks, exit before the wall.

1

Bias: structure plus VWAP

  • Higher highs and higher lows, and price above the session VWAP: you're long for the session. Lower highs, lower lows, under VWAP: short.
  • He's a trend follower. The chance it keeps going is bigger than the chance it breaks structure and turns.
  • Price drifting between the two? That's the middle of a short and a long bias. Wait, or let the heatmap decide.

VWAP is the confirmation: as long as a long trend stays above it, the long idea is still alive.

2

Market: trade the one that's moving

  • Watch NQ and ES side by side. They usually move together, so one confirms the other.
  • If ES is going two points up and two back, switch to NQ (or the other way). You only earn when it moves.
  • London is quieter: smaller moves, thinner books. The US session is faster: wider stops, so smaller size.

Chop is where big players like to fill their orders. Unless you're one of them, it's not your market.

3

Location: the pullback, not the middle

  • The best spot is a pullback to VWAP, ideally where an earlier support level sits too.
  • A wick into VWAP that comes straight back is better than a candle that sits there.
  • Or a small range after a dip (could be accumulation) that breaks back in the trend's direction.

Price likes to go through VWAP before it turns. That's why a first touch isn't enough on its own.

4

Trigger: their push stops working

  • On the heatmap: sellers hit it with big market orders (red bubbles), but the price stops going lower.
  • The bid liquidity under price stays put and doesn't get taken. Small buyers start lifting it, and a green candle closes.
  • Enter on the bid with a one-click limit, not at the ask. You don't pay the spread.

When the market sells hard and can't go lower, that's enough. It went sideways, and sideways after a selloff can be accumulation.

5

Risk: fixed dollars, cut it early

  • The stop is a set dollar amount: about $400 a prop account a trade, which was 20 points on NQ in the London session. Size to the stop.
  • If big sell orders come in and the bid liquidity under you keeps getting taken, get out. Don't wait for the full stop.
  • Don't pin the stop tight or jump to break even right away. A small dip will take you out right before it goes.

His first trade of the session lost about half the planned risk instead of all of it. It's a big difference, he says.

6

Add on pullbacks, out before the wall

  • In profit and it pulls back with the trend still intact? Add size. Move the stop under VWAP.
  • Once the pullback is done and price takes out the local high, the stop can go to break even or a little above.
  • Liquidity above can pull price like a magnet. Take partials into it, and get out a few ticks before a big wall.

"Don't be a dick for a tick." The last few ticks to the wall aren't worth the risk of it holding.

What the heatmap shows

Bookmap or any heatmap that shows the resting liquidity.

Bright bars

Resting limit orders. The brighter the bar, the more contracts sit at that price.

Bubbles

Market orders that filled. Green bubbles bought at the ask, red ones sold at the bid. Bigger bubble, bigger order.

Pulled and moved

Liquidity taken out and put back higher, again and again, in a long trend: follow it and stay in.

Stacked against you

Big red bubbles, then more and more liquidity on the offer: the trade idea is done.

Size it, then add

The stop is a dollar amount first, points second. Put in your risk a trade, the stop in points and the target, then see what a second lot on the pullback does. The defaults are the walkthrough's long.

What he'd tell a newer trader

Smaller size, a bigger stop and a bigger target. Most people get squeezed out by a tight stop right before the move goes their way, and then they get frustrated.

2:1

A fixed reward to risk to start. 5:1 means a low win rate and a lot of frustration.

3 / day

Three trades at most for a day trader. More is chasing.

Months

Stick with one plan for months, not a day or two.

Track two numbers

  • Win rate and profit factor. If one goes up and the other holds, you improved.
  • His: 55 to 60% wins, a profit factor around 1.7 to 1.8. Some weeks day trading it's 1.0 to 1.5, and some weeks he's under break even.
  • A higher reward to risk always means a lower win rate. There's no way around that.

Up on the day? Risk only what keeps it green. Break even is fine; red after green isn't.

How you trade it

Start to finish, for one session.

  1. Bias: before the open, read the structure and VWAP. Higher lows above VWAP: longs only. Lower highs below it: shorts only.
  2. Market: put NQ and ES side by side. Trade the one that's moving; the other one is your confirmation.
  3. Location: wait for a pullback toward VWAP or support, or a small range after the dip. Not the middle of the move.
  4. Trigger: on the heatmap, the other side's big orders stop moving price and the liquidity on your side holds. A candle closes your way. Enter on the bid (or the offer for a short).
  5. Risk: a fixed dollar stop, sized for the session (micros when it's fast). If big orders stack against you, get out early.
  6. Add: on a pullback while you're in profit and the trend holds. Stop under VWAP, then to break even once the local high breaks.
  7. Exit: partials into liquidity, and the rest a few ticks before the big wall. Three trades a day, then done.

The indicator

One TradingView script for the setup (Pine Script v6), with the same rules as our test. A chart can't see the heatmap's resting orders, so it reads the bubbles, the market orders, from TradingView's footprint. It marks and alerts. It does not place trades.

  • Heatmap Scalp

    alerts

    It draws VWAP from the open and shades the chart green when price is above it with higher lows, red below it with lower highs. In that bias, a pullback to VWAP or the last swing low that meets a minute of heavy selling gets a dot. If the next minute holds that low and is net buying, their push failed: it marks a "LONG" with a 20-point stop and a 2:1 target (short: the mirror). Another heavy-selling minute while you're under water marks "Cut early". In profit, a new trigger on a pullback marks "ADD", moves the stop under VWAP, and once price takes out the high before the pullback, moves it a tick above the first entry.

    The bias, the 20-point stop, 2:1, cutting early, adding on pullbacks and three trades a day are from the video. Questions

What does a liquidity heatmap show?

Resting limit orders as bright horizontal bars (brighter means more contracts at that price) and filled market orders as bubbles, green for buyers and red for sellers. It shows where the size is and whether it's being taken or pulled.

When do you get out early?

When big orders stack against the trade: large red bubbles on a long and the bid liquidity under you keeps getting taken. In the video that cut the first loss to about half the planned risk.

When do you add to the trade?

Only on a pullback while you're already in profit and the trend still holds, not on a breakout. The stop for both goes under VWAP, then to just above the first entry once the local high breaks.

Does the Heatmap Scalp work?

We tested the video's rules on a year of NQ futures with real buy and sell orders, and they broke even: 163 trades, 29% wins, −0.01R a trade, against −0.04R for random entries with the same stop and target. The order-flow trigger beat the same pullbacks without it (−0.08R), but the sample is too small to call an edge. The heatmap's resting orders weren't tested. See the test.
