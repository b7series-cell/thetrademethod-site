Watch one entry, start to finish

1

Find the stock

  • Look on the daily chart for a strong stock pulling back to a short-term moving average — mainly the 5 and 10 SMA.
  • This is a scalp. You're in and out, trading the move, not marrying the stock.
  • The whole read runs on two studies: the 9 EMA and VWAP. Nothing else stacked on top.

Fewer inputs means a read you can make the same way every time, under time pressure, without second-guessing it.

2

The stock turns off the low

  • It sells off, puts in a low, and stops going down. That's the starting point — nothing to trade yet.
  • You are not buying here. You're just marking that the low is in and watching what happens next.

Buying the low itself means betting on a turn that hasn't happened. You're still fighting the sellers at that price, not yet moving with anyone.

3

Momentum builds back toward VWAP

  • After the low, price grinds its way back up toward VWAP — the volume-weighted average, the day's center of gravity.
  • You want that push steady, not violent. Steady says the buying is consistent and likely to keep going.

This is momentum rebuilding in real time, in front of you. It's the proof you're waiting for, not the turn itself.

4

The trigger: 9 EMA crosses VWAP

  • The 9 EMA is fast and hugs price. When it turns up and crosses through VWAP, that is the signal.
  • Same trade in reverse for shorts — the fast line crossing down through a flat or falling VWAP.

The cross is the moment the fast line and the day's average finally agree on direction. Before that, you're only guessing which way it breaks.

5

Enter right at the cross

  • You enter at the cross. Not after the candle closes, not three bars later once you feel sure.
  • The cross is the confirmation. It happens, you're in.

Waiting for more confirmation than the cross itself just means paying up for the same trade after the cheap part of the move is gone.

6

Stop and target off the measured move

  • Measure the distance from the low of day up to the cross. That distance is your one unit.
  • Target is one full unit above the cross. Stop is a third of the way back down toward the low.
  • Because you enter right around VWAP, the low-to-cross distance and the risk-to-stop distance land close to the same ratio — roughly 3 to 1, risk one to make three.

The math comes out clean because the level does the work for you. You're not picking a stop distance, you're reading it off the move that already happened.

Low of day, the grind back to VWAP, the 9 EMA cross that triggers the entry, and the measured-move stop and target.

Not every cross is worth trusting

The 9 EMA crossing VWAP is the trigger, but the character of that cross tells you how much to trust it.

Two windows, and only two

Same setup, same math, but time of day is part of the filter, not a footnote.

Morning · 10:00–10:45

The first pocket. Enough movement and participation right after the open settles down. (The indicator opens this window at 9:45.)

Midday · 10:46–1:30

The second pocket. Same setup fires again once midday flow picks back up.

Everywhere else

Same read, same cross, same math — but outside these windows it under-performs. Skip it.

The four filters

The cross gets you in, but not every cross is the same. These decide how much to trust the one in front of you.

1  Speed off the low

A steady, clean, committed push back toward VWAP — not a violent spike. Steady says the buying is real and likely to keep going.

Kills: climactic spikes that snap back.

2  Hold above VWAP

Once price crosses back above VWAP, it needs to hold there. Choppy, directionless action after the cross is noise, not signal.

Kills: chop mistaken for a breakout.

3  No stall at the cross

If price just sits there for 10–15 minutes right at the cross instead of going, that hesitation says the commitment isn't there yet.

Kills: forcing a trade that's already stalled.

4  The 9 EMA can't be flat

If the 9 EMA goes completely flat before you get your entry, the momentum that was supposed to carry the trade already stalled out.

Kills: late entries on a dead move.

The low-to-cross distance is your one unit. Skip that math and eyeball a flat stop instead, and your real risk stops matching your budget.

The mistake this prevents

Skip the measured move and eyeball a flat dollar stop instead — say, $1 — and $500 ÷ $1 says buy 500 shares.

500 × $1.50 = $750 at the trade's actual stop.

Fifty percent over budget, because the flat number never matched the move this particular stock actually made.

You're not buying the bottom. You're buying proof it already happened.

Guessing the turn is how you catch a falling knife on the way down. Waiting for the cross is how you catch the move once it's already turned.

  • A flat 9 EMA or ten minutes of chop after the cross means the move isn't there yet — skip it, don't force it.
  • Outside the two windows, the identical setup on the identical stock is a worse trade. Time of day is a filter, not a footnote.
  • This setup wins roughly 6 times in 10. The edge is the payoff ratio holding up over many trades, not any single one of them.
