Watch one CRT long, start to finish

1

Set the bias on a higher timeframe first

  • Before looking for a single CRT trigger, mark the most extreme recent swing high or low on a higher timeframe and wait for price to sweep that level and reverse away from it.
  • Only once that higher-timeframe sweep-and-reversal has actually confirmed does a direction become tradeable — CRTs are taken with that bias, never against it.

A three-candle trigger without a confirmed higher-timeframe direction is just a pattern — the bias is what turns it into a trade.

2

Candle 1 marks the extreme — and nothing else

  • On the 1-hour, 30-minute, or 15-minute chart only, let one candle close and mark its high and low. That range is a reference level, not a signal to act on by itself.
  • The 1-minute chart never builds this candle — it has no role in forming the setup, only in timing an entry once the setup already exists.

Skip this step and there's no fixed range for candle 2 to sweep — the whole sequence loses its reference point.

3

Candle 2 is the sweep, not a breakout

  • The next candle has to wick beyond one side of candle 1's range — below the low for a long setup, above the high for a short — and then close back inside that range.
  • If it closes beyond the range instead of snapping back inside it, the setup is void. That's a breakout in the wrong structure, not a sweep.

The wick-and-close-back-in is what shows liquidity being taken and rejected. A candle that just closes through the level is trend continuation, not a CRT.

4

Candle 3 is the trigger — and it has a clock on it

  • Enter only when price breaks candle 2's high (long) or candle 2's low (short). Nothing before that break is a valid entry, no matter how convincing the sweep looked.
  • If candle 3 closes without breaking that level, the setup is dead. A fourth or later candle breaking the same level afterward is considered too low-quality to take.

Without the clock, "wait for the break" quietly turns into "wait indefinitely" — and a stale level breaking three or four candles later isn't the same trade anymore.

5

Choose the entry style before candle 3 forms

  • Direct break: enter the instant candle 3 crosses candle 2's level. More trades trigger, at a lower win rate.
  • Rebreak: wait for the initial break, a pullback, and a second break before entering — timed on the 1-minute chart. Fewer trades trigger, at a higher win rate.

Both are the same setup with a different entry filter layered on top — the choice is a trade-frequency decision, not a separate strategy.

6

Target first, then drag the stop to exactly 1.5R

  • The take-profit goes on first, at the opposing extreme — the higher-timeframe swing level the bias in step one was measured against.
  • The stop-loss is not a fixed point distance. It's dragged to whatever distance makes the trade exactly 1.5 times reward-to-risk, recalculated on every single trade.

Fix the stop first instead, and reward-to-risk becomes whatever the market happens to give you — the entire point of this setup is that it never is.

Candle 1 marks the extreme, candle 2 sweeps it and closes back inside, candle 3 breaks candle 2's high to trigger the long — target at the opposing extreme, stop dragged to exactly 1.5R.

The four filters

The three candles don't change — these are what keep you from forcing the trigger onto a chart, a day, or a timeframe it was never built for.

1  Building the setup on a 1-minute chart

The three candles only form on the 1-hour, 30-minute, or 15-minute chart. The 1-minute has exactly two jobs: timing a rebreak entry, and reading whether the tape is too choppy to trade.

Kills: a "setup" that was never actually built on a timeframe this trigger works on.

2  No higher-timeframe bias confirmed

Taking a CRT trigger before the higher-timeframe extreme has actually been swept and reversed — trading the pattern without the direction it depends on.

Kills: a mechanically correct trigger fighting the wrong side of the higher-timeframe move.

3  Low-volume, no-catalyst days

Especially Mondays — thin participation makes a sweep-and-reject mean far less, since there's no real liquidity being taken in the first place.

Kills: a technically valid sweep that has no real order flow behind it.

4  Choppy price action

Alternating massive-body and massive-wick candles with no follow-through between them — the market not settling into a real directional read.

Kills: a break that has nowhere to go because the tape has no follow-through left in it.

The mistake this prevents

Setting the stop first — off a fixed number of points, or wherever feels comfortable — and only then seeing what reward-to-risk that happens to produce.

On a setup built around exactly 1.5R, a stop-first process turns disciplined math into a guess that drifts trade to trade.

Set the target at the opposing extreme first. Let the ratio — not a feeling — decide exactly where the stop sits.

Mechanical trigger. Mechanical risk.

The setup is the same three candles every time, and the risk is the same exact ratio every time — there's no discretion left to second-guess once the trigger actually prints.

  • New York AM only, built on the 1-hour, 30-minute, or 15-minute chart — never build the three candles on a 1-minute chart.
  • Bias, then sweep, then break, in that order — skip the days with no real participation and the tape that won't settle.
  • Target first, stop dragged to force exactly 1.5R — never the other way around.
