Liquidity Refill

When buyers take liquidity faster than sellers can add it back, price has only one way to go. You can't see the order book, but you can see its footprint: a big candle out of compression, then a next candle whose early sell-off gets absorbed in seconds, high up in the first candle. Buy the break of the first candle's high, stop under the wick, and get out after three or four candles.

What is a liquidity refill setup?

A liquidity refill is a two-candle pattern from a quant trader's order book research. After a relatively big candle (C1), the next candle (C2) dips early and shallow, in the top 10 to 20% of C1, and gets absorbed fast. Buy when C2 breaks C1's high, stop past the wick, and exit after three or four candles.

Watch one liquidity refill long, start to finish

1

Start from compression

  • A stretch of small candles: a low-volatility state, ideally with a simple trend line or market structure you can see.
  • It's what makes the next candle stand out. The candles closest to it should be clearly smaller.
  • Skip choppy charts where big candles of about the same size keep coming in a short stretch.

Compression, then an imbalance candle, is where this gives the most accurate results.

2

Spot C1, a relatively big candle

  • Bigger than the candles around it. Call it whatever you like; it's just a relatively big candle.
  • Assume one side was aggressive: buyers took liquidity at a much higher rate than sellers could add it back (or the reverse, short).
  • A structural break at the same time, through a trend line or a few prior highs, makes it more interesting. It isn't required.

One candle isn't enough to know there's an imbalance. It's the first assumption. What happens next decides it.

3

Watch the first seconds of C2

  • For a long, a quick sell-off right as C2 opens. On a 1-minute chart, in the first 10 to 15 seconds.
  • The wick stays small: in the top 10 to 15% of C1 on the whiteboard, and no deeper than 20%. It shouldn't come near C1's 50% level.
  • Then buyers absorb it and turn aggressive again: the candle flips. For a short it's all mirrored: a quick pop, absorbed, near C1's low.

Buyers are now lining up liquidity at a much higher price than before, and doing it quickly enough to absorb the selling instantly.

4

Enter when C2 crosses C1's high

  • Long: when price crosses above C1's high. Short: when it crosses below C1's low.
  • It has to be C2. If C2 doesn't break it and C3 does, there's no trade.
  • C2 has to be on your side: bullish for a long. A small wick on a bearish C2 is no trade.

C1's high is the highest price buyers paid. Crossing it says they're now willing to do price discovery above it.

5

Stop just past the wick

  • Long: a little below C2's wick. Short: a little above it.
  • Roughly, not to the tick. Philip puts it a bit away from the wick; you can put it right at it.
  • If the wick is too big, the stop is too wide for the trade to pay. Consider skipping it.

The tight stop is the whole idea. You can lose many times; when it works the win makes it all back.

6

Ride three or four candles, then out

  • An imbalance only lasts a few candles. Out after three or four, as long as each closes in your direction.
  • Out early on the first opposite candle: a red close on a long, a green one on a short. The imbalance is gone.
  • Out earlier at an obvious level in the way, like the previous day's low or high.

Don't expect 20 candles. It's a quick scalp: you want at least 1:2, most likely 1:3.

When to skip it

Simple, but not on every chart.

Chop

Big candles of about the same size, one after another in a short stretch. No compression, no clear structure break.

Ignore the wick entirely.

A late wick

The sell-off (or pop, short) came later in C2, not in its first moments. That's not the absorption you're looking for.

Late wick: no trade.

A wick too deep

It goes past C1's 50% level, or far enough that the stop behind it leaves no room for 1:2.

Risk-reward gone: pass.

No break on C2

C2 closes against you or never crosses C1's high (low). C3 breaking it instead doesn't count.

C1, C2. Not C3.

Aggression meets refill

Two ideas from order book microstructure. Aggression is who's taking liquidity: buyers crossing the spread to take what the ask offers. Liquidity refill is the rate at which new limit orders are added back as the old ones get consumed. Together they show an imbalance, and an imbalance moves price. Traders who can see the tape read the same thing on a footprint (order flow, stacked imbalances) or a heatmap (Heatmap Scalp); this setup reads it from two candles.

The whiteboard example

Buyers are willing to buy 10,000 contracts a second by crossing the spread. Sellers can only add back 2,000 a second. The ask is refilling, but at a much slower rate, and buyers easily consume whatever comes back.

If that continues, there's only one way price can go: up.

Most traders don't have this data, and most order execution today is automated. You don't need it: the next section is how the same imbalance shows up on a plain price chart. The 4,000 contracts a level in the sketch is ours, just to make it move.

What C2 has to do

Same compression, same C1. Only the second candle changes. The wick has to come early, stay shallow, and C2 itself has to flip and break C1's high. The strip under each chart is C2's first minute, with the first 15 seconds in green.

In the live NASDAQ trade, the first imbalance candle was skipped for exactly the third reason: the wick on the next candle formed later in its 15 minutes, so it wasn't marked.

Grade the wick, then size it

The wick is your risk

The smaller the wick, the closer the stop, the better the trade. Inside the top 5% of C1 is an even stronger case than 20%. Past 50%, it isn't the pattern any more.

At least 1:2. Most likely 1:3.

The price has to make a pretty big move to get there, so if the wick is a bit too big and throws off the risk-reward, Philip would consider not taking it, even with everything else in place.

Missed it? The second chance

If you spot the pattern on a higher time frame and miss the break, drop to a lower one. As long as price stays above C2's low, the initial wick, look for a retest of C2's opening level. When price closes back above the open, that can be a second entry, often with a tighter stop.

The rules for the retest

  • C2's low, the initial wick, must not be violated. That's also where the first trade's stop is.
  • Price has to come back and test C2's opening level. It doesn't have to go far below it: a quick bounce or a rejection wick is fine. Be a little open-minded.
  • When price closes back above the open, that's the second entry, with the stop under the retest.
  • One retest only. If it comes back a second time, the imbalance most likely isn't in place.

Skip it on dead candles

If the retest happens on very low volatility candles, ignore it. Technical analysis works best with participants in the market and real volatility.

Philip takes the initial entry, not the retest.

“If I miss the trade, I'm fine. I move on to the next day.” He doesn't want to spend hours watching the chart or chase it.

Three ways out

It's a quick setup. You don't need to decide how long to hold it: count candles and watch their color, and know where the obvious level is.

01

Three or four candles

Each one closing higher than the last on a long (lower, short). After three or four, you're out. The move most likely pulls back next.

02

The first opposite candle

If a candle closes red after your long entry (green after a short), close it. The imbalance isn't in place any more.

03

An obvious level

Like the previous day's low in the live short. Levels everyone can see tend to get retested and respected, often with a bounce, before a break.

How you trade it

The whole setup, in order.

  1. Find compression: small candles, low volatility, ideally a simple trend line or structure. Skip chop.
  2. Spot C1, a relatively big candle. Assume aggression is outrunning the refill.
  3. Watch the first moments of C2: a quick sell-off (pop, short) that stays in C1's top 10–20% and gets absorbed. Late or deep: no trade.
  4. Enter when C2 crosses C1's high (low, short). Only C2, and only if C2 is on your side.
  5. Stop just past C2's wick. If that makes the risk too big for 1:2, consider passing.
  6. Out after three or four candles, on the first opposite candle, or at an obvious level like the previous day's low or high.
  7. Missed it? One retest of C2's open on a lower time frame, above C2's low, with real volatility. Or just move on.

Questions

What does liquidity refill mean?

It's the rate at which new limit orders are added back as old ones get taken. When buyers take liquidity faster than sellers can refill it, price has only one way to go.

How early does the C2 wick have to come?

On a 1-minute chart, in the first 10 to 15 seconds of C2. On a 5-minute chart roughly the first minute, on a 15-minute chart the first couple of minutes, and on a daily chart the first few hours.

When should you skip a liquidity refill?

In chop with same-size big candles, when the wick comes late in C2, when it goes past C1's 50% level, or when C2 never breaks C1's high. A break on C3 doesn't count.

Does the liquidity refill strategy work?

Not in our test. We coded the video's rules on a year of NQ futures, using every trade to time the wick to the second (9:30 to noon ET, Oct 2025 to Nov 2026). On the 1-minute chart, 193 trades lost 0.36R each, worse than random entries; 5-minute made +0.15R on 64 trades and 15-minute lost 0.07R on 65. Most trades are stopped out: the wins are rare and big.
