What is the Gamma Wall Footprint?

The Gamma Wall Footprint is a set of three NQ futures day trades at the levels from a GEX (gamma exposure) map: the put wall, the call wall and zero gamma. The wall tells you where, not when. A slow drift into a wall means look for the reaction right at it; a fast push means let it overshoot and enter on the reclaim. The footprint chart confirms the entry with absorption, stacked imbalances, a delta flip or trapped traders. The third setup goes with the move when price breaks below zero gamma and holds.

1

Map: put wall, call wall, zero gamma

  • Add up the gamma exposure of every strike and you get the GEX map. Three lines on it matter.
  • The call wall, the most positive gamma: dealers sell rallies there. A ceiling with springs.
  • The put wall, the most negative gamma: dealers buy dips there. A trampoline floor.
  • Zero gamma isn't support or resistance. It's the border between two kinds of market.

Price doesn't react at random prices. It reacts where the dealers' hedging is biggest, and you can know those prices before the open.

2

Regime: the lake or the rapids

  • Above zero gamma, dealers sell rips and buy dips. Their hedging soaks up moves: a calm lake, you paddle back and forth.
  • Below it, they sell weakness and buy strength. Their hedging adds fuel: the rapids, and the current takes you.
  • Before any trade, ask one question: am I on the lake or in the rapids?

Same-day (0DTE) options make this strongest in the first hours of the US session. One or two good setups, then you're done.

3

Approach: how price arrives

  • Slow approach: small candles, a steady drift. Look for the reaction right at the level.
  • Fast approach: big candles, real momentum. Don't catch the ball while it's stretching the net.
  • Let a fast move overshoot, then enter when price gets back to the level. That's the reclaim, and it proves the breakout failed.

Throw a ball softly at a net and it stops right there. Throw it hard and it stretches way past the line, then snaps back.

4

Confirm: the footprint knocks

  • Absorption: sellers keep hitting it, the red numbers pile up, and price won't go lower.
  • Stacked imbalances: three rows or more where one side is three times bigger. Someone just took control.
  • A delta flip: big negative, then big positive, right at the level.
  • Trapped traders: they rushed through the door just before it slammed shut behind them.

GEX shows you where the door is. The footprint tells you if someone is knocking.

5

Risk: stop where the idea is wrong

  • Ask: if this wall is supposed to hold, where would price have to go to prove me wrong? On NQ that's often 30 to 50 ticks.
  • The target is the next level. Small stop, big target, but only if you enter near the level.
  • Same wall, same target: enter close and it's 8R. Chase it late and it's not even 1R.

The level gives you a short distance to being wrong. Enter far from it and you've thrown that away.

6

Manage: half off, the rest rides

  • With more than one contract, take some off at the first reaction and let the rest ride to the next level.
  • If a wall breaks and price accepts beyond it, don't stand in front of a broken dam holding a bucket. Reassess.
  • Check the calendar: OPEX, triple witching and VIX expiration move the levels. Check yourself after a loss.

On moving day all the furniture is in the hallway. Your usual levels might not be where you left them.

Three ways to find the levels

Three models, one rule: location first, execution second.

Model 1Gamma walls

The call wall, the put wall, and the second biggest ones behind them. That's your map for the day.

Model 2Open interest

Volume is everyone who walked past the stadium today. Open interest is the crowd still in their seats. Look back about 90 days; the biggest crowds become levels.

Model 3Convexity

Stepping stones across a river. Positive convexity pockets are the stones, and price likes to travel from one to the next.

When to leave it alone

Any one of these and it isn't a trade yet.

One big print

A huge call trade on a flow scanner. It could be a hedge, one leg of a spread, or a bet on volatility.

One trade without context tells you almost nothing.

Just a wall

Price is at the level, but the footprint shows nobody knocking. Or a fast move is still stretching the net.

A broken dam

The wall broke and price accepted beyond it.

Don't fade it with a bucket. Reassess.

Moving day, or angry

OPEX, triple witching or VIX expiration. Or the trade right after a loss.

Read the footprint

A footprint splits every candle into price rows: contracts sold aggressively at the bid on the left, bought aggressively at the ask on the right. Ask minus bid is delta. Pick a sign to see it.

A gamma wall alone is information. A big futures trade alone is information. Price action alone is information. Stack them at the same price and you have a trade.The video's closing point, in our words.

How you trade it

Start to finish, for one morning.

  1. Map: before the open, mark the put wall, the call wall and zero gamma (and the second biggest walls). Open interest and convexity pockets can add levels.
  2. Regime: ask if you're on the lake (above zero gamma, moves get absorbed) or in the rapids (below it, moves stretch). If price breaks below zero gamma and holds, switch to the third play and go with it.
  3. Approach: watch how price arrives at the level. Slow drift: look for the reaction at the level. Fast push: let it overshoot and wait for the reclaim.
  4. Confirm: on the footprint, at least one of absorption, stacked imbalances, a delta flip or trapped traders, right at the level.
  5. Risk: stop where the wall idea is proven wrong (often 30 to 50 ticks on NQ), target the next level. Only take it if you're close to the level.
  6. Manage: half off at the first reaction, the rest to the next level. A wall that breaks and holds is a reason to reassess, not to add.
  7. Stop: one or two quality setups in the first hours, then done. Recheck the map on expiration days, and check yourself after a loss.

Questions

What are the call wall, put wall and zero gamma?

The call wall is the strike with the most positive gamma, where dealers tend to sell rallies. The put wall is the strike with the most negative gamma, where dealers tend to buy dips. Zero gamma is the border: above it dealer hedging absorbs moves, below it the hedging adds fuel and moves stretch.

Why not just buy or sell at the wall?

Because the wall tells you where, not when. Price can run straight through it, especially on a fast approach. You wait for the footprint to show someone defending the level, or for a fast move to overshoot and come back (the reclaim).

Where does the stop go?

Where the wall idea is proven wrong: past the failed low or the overshoot high, often 30 to 50 ticks on NQ. The target is the next level. Entering close to the level is what makes the reward big: in the video's example, entering at the wall was about 8R, chasing it later was under 1R.

When should you not trade the levels?

On a single big options print without context, when a wall has broken and price accepted beyond it, on OPEX, triple witching and VIX expiration days when the levels move, and right after a loss if the next trade isn't from the plan.
