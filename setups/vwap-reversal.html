VWAP Reversal

What is a VWAP reversal?

A VWAP reversal is a fade that stacks three signals at one price: a touch of the second VWAP band, an old horizontal level from the higher time frame, and a failure test that prints a reversal candle. You enter at that candle's close, with the stop just past the pattern's extreme and about 2:1 reward to risk.

1

Build the VWAP with its second band

  • Add the built-in VWAP indicator, then turn on a second bands multiplier in its settings so you get an outer band, not just the median line.
  • Set the band calculation to standard deviation, and thicken the lines so the bands are easy to read at a glance.

The median VWAP line alone is just an average. The second band is what turns it into something you can actually treat as support or resistance — a level price has to work to reach.

2

Mark the old level before you zoom in

  • Before dropping into the intraday chart, check the higher time frame for a level that already matters — an old high, an old low, anywhere the stock has shown real selling or buying pressure before.
  • Draw that level out as a horizontal line. This is market structure, not something you invent on the fly once price gets close.

Levels that are highly visible — old highs especially — are levels many other traders are already watching. That's what gives a horizontal level real weight instead of being just a line you drew.

3

Wait for the band touch at that level

  • The setup starts when price reaches the second VWAP band and that touch happens at or near the horizontal level you already marked.
  • A band touch by itself is just a statistical extension. A band touch at a level the market already remembers is two signals stacking at once.

Dynamic support/resistance (the band) and static support/resistance (the old level) rarely agree by accident. When they do, that's the first real signal, not a random touch.

4

Let the failure test set the trap

  • Watch for price to briefly push through the level and then immediately fall back below it — a failure test.
  • That push traps traders betting on a breakout continuation; their stop-losses stack just beyond the level, and when price reverses, those stops add fuel to the move.

Smart selling into strength plus a wave of forced stop-loss selling is what makes a failure test move faster than a plain reversal off a level that was never actually tested.

5

Confirm with the candle, enter on its close

  • The failure test should print a clear reversal candle — a shooting star at resistance, a hammer at support. That candle is the third signal.
  • Enter at the close of that candle, not before it's finished. The pattern isn't confirmed until the candle is actually done printing.

All three signals stacking — the band, the old level, and the confirmed reversal candle — is what separates a real trade from a guess dressed up as one.

6

Stop above the extreme, size for 2:1

  • Set the stop just beyond the high (or low, on the long side) of the failure-test pattern — the extreme point, not an arbitrary distance from entry.
  • Keep the reward-to-risk simple to start: aim for roughly twice what you're risking, and don't force a trade into a session that never actually gave you all three signals.

A defined stop against the pattern's own extreme lets you size the trade properly. Forcing a version of this that's missing a signal is how a clean method turns into a guess again.

Which touch you can trust

The band touches this level constantly. What actually stacks with it — or doesn't — is what separates a real trade from a random one.

Why three, not one

Each signal comes from a different source. That's what makes them worth stacking instead of trading on their own.

Signal 1 & 2 — the band and the old level

One is dynamic: a live, volume-weighted statistical extension. One is static: a level the market has already reacted to before. They rarely line up by accident.

Signal 3 — the failure test

Price pushes through, traps late breakout buyers, then falls back below. Their stop-losses, stacked just beyond the break, add fuel once it reverses.

The trigger — the reversal candle

A shooting star at resistance, a hammer at support. It confirms the failure test and sets the exact entry: at its close, not before.

The four filters

These don't find the trade — the three stacked signals do that. They keep you off the versions that only look like it.

1  Band touch with no old level behind it

A stray touch of the second band, with nothing on the higher time frame that says the market has ever cared about this price before.

Kills: mistaking a purely statistical extension for real resistance.

2  No failure test

Price touches the level and reverses cleanly, without ever pushing through and trapping anyone first.

Kills: entering on a touch that never actually set the trap.

3  Entering before the candle closes

Betting on the reversal while the candle is still printing, instead of waiting for it to actually confirm.

Kills: getting run over by a continuation that never fails.

4  Forcing it on a slow day

Scanning until you find something close enough instead of waiting for all three signals to genuinely stack.

Kills: trading a lookalike instead of the real setup.

What the VWAP band actually measures

The median line and the band around it come from two different ideas — knowing which is which is why the second band earns a place in this setup.

The VWAP line itself is the volume-weighted average price for the session — not a simple average of price over time, but an average weighted by how much actually traded at each price. It resets every session and is often treated as the price the market considers "fair" for the day so far. The bands built around it are standard deviations of price away from that average — a statistical read on how far the current move has stretched relative to typical trading around the session's volume-weighted center. A touch of the plain VWAP line just means price is near the session average. A touch of the second band means price has stretched further than usual — which is what makes it worth treating as a real level, especially the moment it lines up with a horizontal level the market already remembers.

Questions

What settings does the VWAP need?

The built-in VWAP with a second bands multiplier turned on, calculated by standard deviation, so there is an outer band and not just the median line.

What is a failure test?

Price briefly pushes through the level and then falls straight back. It traps breakout buyers, and their stops just beyond the level add fuel to the reversal.

Which candle confirms it?

A shooting star at resistance or a hammer at support. Enter at its close, not while it is still printing.

Does the VWAP reversal work?

Not in our test: 11,090 trades on 5-minute bars came out just under break-even, with a 39% win rate, a 0.98 profit factor and -0.01R per trade.

One signal is a guess. Three is a trade.

The band, the old level, and the failure-test candle each come from a different source. When they stack at the same price, that's not a coincidence worth ignoring — it's the trade.

  • Mark your levels before you zoom in. The band touch means more when it lines up with structure the market already respects.
  • Wait for the failure test and the confirming candle. Enter at the close, not on the touch.
  • Don't force a version of this that's missing a signal. Scan more charts instead of lowering the bar on this one.
