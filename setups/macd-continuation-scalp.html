MACD Continuation Scalp

Watch one MACD continuation scalp

1

Find the flat consolidation first

  • Look for a period — roughly 3 to 15 minutes on a 1-minute chart — where the MACD line sits wrapped tightly around the zero line. That means the fast and slow moving averages are converging: nothing extended, energy building up.
  • This step is just identifying the setup, not a signal to act. You're not anticipating a break; you're marking a spot worth watching.

Zero means the moving averages are neither extended up nor down — locked and loaded. Skip this step and you're trading a cross with no real energy built up behind it, which just produces chop.

2

Watch for the expansion

  • Once price starts to move, the MACD line shoots away from zero as the two moving averages diverge — that's the momentum move actually starting.
  • You're still not in the trade. You're watching the divergence happen and letting it develop.

The expansion is what tells you the consolidation actually produced something. Buying the initial break itself, before this confirms, is exactly the anticipation this trade is designed to remove.

3

Let it pull back and hold its side of zero

  • After the expansion, the MACD line curls back toward its own signal line — a natural pause after the move.
  • The setup only stays valid if both lines remain on the same side of zero the whole time: above zero throughout for a long, below zero throughout for a short. A cross that straddles the zero line isn't this trade.

Staying on the correct side of zero through the pullback is what keeps you trading with the trend that's already established, instead of guessing at a turn.

4

Enter on the cross back

  • The trigger is the fast MACD line crossing back above its signal line while both are still above zero (mirror it below zero for a short).
  • Stop goes just below the low of that pullback in price — the same little dip that produced the MACD's curl back toward its signal line.

You don't have to predict the turn. The cross itself, confirmed above (or below) zero, is the signal that the pause is over and the original move is resuming.

5

Let the lines manage the trade

  • Stay in as long as the fast line remains on the favorable side of the signal line — above it for a long, below it for a short.
  • The moment the lines cross back the other way, that's the exit. No predicting how far it goes, no anticipating the top.

Letting go of the need to manage price directly is the entire point of this trade — the indicator makes the stay-or-go decision so you don't have to guess in real time.

6

Pick the right conditions, and know when it's over

  • This works best on a trending or relative-strength name, something breaking out of a higher-time-frame range, or a stock consolidating intraday — not a random, choppy ticker with no context.
  • Once a name has already run through the full sequence once, don't chase the next cross on the same move. That setup is finished; wait for a fresh consolidation to build.

The setup needs real energy built up first. Forcing it on a stock with no consolidation, or forcing a second trade out of a move that already played out, is how this stops being a high-probability trade.

Reading the MACD without the histogram

Custom settings, and one piece of the standard indicator this setup deliberately ignores.

The MACD line

The spread between a 3-period and a 9-period moving average here — not the standard 12/26.

The signal line

A 5-period moving average of that spread — not the standard 9. Crosses between the two are the trigger.

No histogram

This setup only reads the line-to-line and line-to-zero relationship. The histogram isn't part of the trigger.

The four filters

These don't find the trade — the consolidation and the cross do that. They keep you off the versions that only look like it.

1  No real consolidation first

If the MACD never wraps flat around zero, there's no energy built up behind the eventual cross.

Kills: trading a cross with too much underlying variability.

2  Cross straddles the zero line

Both lines need to be confirmed on the same side of zero — a cross with one line above and one below isn't this trade.

Kills: taking a signal that was never actually confirmed.

3  Chasing a cross after the move already played out

Once a name has run the full sequence once, the next cross on that same move is a forced trade, not a fresh setup.

Kills: manufacturing a second trade out of a finished one.

4  Using the histogram as a signal

This setup is built entirely on the line-to-line and line-to-zero read — the histogram isn't part of it.

Kills: adding a signal this setup was never designed to use.

The mistake this prevents

Take every cross the MACD prints through the day, including the ones with no flat consolidation in front of them.

Without that energy built up first, you get chopped by crosses that reverse almost immediately — small losses that add up fast.

The consolidation is what makes a cross worth trading. Without it, a cross is just noise.

Make the game easy.

This isn't meant to be a home run. It's a base hit — a way to get your rhythm and confidence back with very clear rules, so you can get back to the rest of your playbook.

  • Consolidation, expansion, pullback, cross. Skip the first step and every cross after it is just noise.
  • Both lines have to confirm on the same side of zero — a straddled cross isn't a signal, it's a coin flip.
  • Once the sequence has already played out on a name, let it go. Wait for the next fresh consolidation instead of forcing one.
