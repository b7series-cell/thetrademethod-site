A support or resistance line is only worth trading if real volume changed hands there. 1

Build the map

  • Take the last 600 bars, find their highest high and lowest low, and split that range into 72 equal rows. Each row gets one horizontal bar: the volume traded at those prices.
  • Every bar is split in two: estimated buying volume (teal) and estimated selling volume (indigo). The longest row is the point of control (POC), marked in orange.

Long rows are prices where the market did a lot of business, and those are the prices it tends to stop at again. The buy/sell split tells you which side did the business: in a downtrend, the rows going down are mostly selling.

2

Only trade levels the volume earned

  • The indicator finds swing highs and lows (five bars either side), then checks the profile near each one. Only a swing with a real volume node close by becomes a level: red for resistance, green for support.
  • A swing is only confirmed five bars after it prints, so the level starts there — it was never tradeable on the swing bar itself.

A swing point on its own is just where price turned once. A swing point sitting on heavy volume is where a lot of positions were opened, and the people holding them tend to defend it.

3

Read the strength

  • The thicker the line and the bigger the rings, the more volume traded around that swing, and the stronger the level.
  • Each level carries a 0–100 score built from the volume node, heavy volume on small candles near the swing, the swing bar's own volume, and its rejection wick. Up to 20 levels stay active; weaker ones drop off and near-duplicates merge.

The score ranks levels against each other. It is not the odds of the level holding — a 90 can break and a 40 can hold.

4

Look for the POC on a level

  • When the point of control sits on or near an identified level, that zone is the strongest on the chart: two separate reads of volume agree on one price.
  • Use it both ways. Fade into it — and when you're already in a trade heading toward it, it's your target. Long into a resistance-plus-POC zone? Take profit just under it.

Price rarely slices straight through the busiest price on the chart when a swing level sits on it too. Expect it to stall there, whichever side you're on.

5

Watch the volume balance on the approach

  • The volume pane splits every bar into estimated buying and selling. Price climbing into resistance on mostly buying, then the balance flipping to selling right at the level, is the market telling you sellers are stepping in.
  • The indicator's version: the winning side holds at least 60% of the bar's volume, and its share is up at least 15 points on the previous three bars.

Price tells you where you are. Volume tells you who's winning there. A level touch with the volume still on the wrong side is price drifting, not rejecting.

6

Take the rejection on the close

  • Buy: price touches support (within 0.15 ATR), the bar closes back above it, the candle is green, and buying is in control with the shift above. Sell: the mirror image at resistance.
  • Target the next opposing level or a POC zone. The level is dead when a bar closes through it by 0.10 ATR — it comes off the chart and does not flip sides.

The map: profile, levels, strength

Levels come from swing points, but only the ones sitting on real volume survive. The heavier the volume, the thicker the line.

The confirmation: the balance flips at the level

Price climbs into resistance on buying. At the level, selling takes over, and the candle closes back underneath.

Which levels count

Every swing point is a candidate. The profile decides which ones become levels.

A level the volume earned — trade this

A swing high or low with a heavy volume node next to it. Drawn as a line with rings and a score; the thicker and bigger, the stronger.

A swing point on its own — skip it

Price turned there once, but little traded nearby. Nobody has a position to defend, so there's no reason for price to react the next time.

The four filters

The levels and the volume pane are always on screen. These are the ways to read them wrong.

1  A swing with nothing under it

Drawing your own line at every swing high and low, instead of trading only the levels the profile backs.

Kills: levels nobody is defending.

2  Deciding on the forming candle

The table shows the live candle's buying and selling as it forms. Useful for watching — but the balance can flip before the close.

Kills: entries on a wick that closes back through the level.

3  A touch without the shift

Price reaches the level, but the volume balance hasn't moved — or it's still with the side you're fading.

Kills: buying support while sellers are still in charge.

4  Entering straight into the POC zone

Going long just under a resistance-plus-POC zone, or short just above a support-plus-POC zone. That zone is a target, not an entry.

Kills: trades with no room to work.

The mistake this prevents

Treating the buy/sell split as real order flow. It's an estimate from where each candle closed inside its own range, not bid-and-ask data.

A candle that closes at its high counts as 100% buying, whoever actually traded it.

Read it as pressure at a level you already trust. Never use the volume split on its own.

The level is the location. The volume is the evidence.

Price can reach any line on the chart. The trade is the line where volume already traded, and where the volume balance turns your way on the close.

  • Trade only the levels the profile backs. A bare swing point is not a level.
  • A POC on a level makes the strongest zone on the chart — fade it, or take profit into it.
  • Wait for the close. Touch, close back on the right side, volume flipped — all three, or no trade.
