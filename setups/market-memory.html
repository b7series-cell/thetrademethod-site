Market Memory

Yesterday's real buyers and real sellers left a map. Not indicators. Not predictions. Not what should happen next. Price comes back to where the market already made its decisions, and this page exists to tell you which reaction to trust and which one to fade.

Watch one day two trade

1

Find a day two name

  • Look for a stock that made a significant move yesterday, ideally with a catalyst — a news story, earnings, or a technical break or failure of a key range.
  • Both count as catalysts because they force other participants to make real decisions about their positions.
  • This is not a scan-everything strategy. Run it on a random name with no prior-day story and you're just trading the noise.

A stock without a real prior-day fight has no memory yet. There's nothing here for price to react to tomorrow.

2

Draw the box

  • Mark yesterday's high and yesterday's low.
  • On the intraday chart, mark the one or two places where price fought hard and lost — an area it pushed into and never got back above, or broke below and never got back under.
  • Cap it at three to four lines total. More than that and you're drawing noise, not memory.

Every line should be a real decision you can point to, not a guess. If you can't name the fight that made the line, don't draw it.

3

Read the box

  • The top of the box is the largest collective seller from the prior day — the price where the market said "no more."
  • The bottom of the box is the largest collective buyer — where real money stepped in and defended the price.
  • Any lines in between are inflection points, not as important as the outer edges. They mark where buyers or sellers defended a level, not just where they first showed up.

These aren't opinions, they're documented decisions. When price returns to them, the people who made those decisions are usually still there.

4

The two mistakes that cost the most

  • Don't buy when price opens or runs quickly into, at, or just above yesterday's high without sustaining above it.
  • Don't sell when price opens or runs quickly into, at, or below yesterday's low without sustaining below it.
  • Don't diddle in the middle. That's where memory is weakest and emotion takes over.

FOMO buys the top of yesterday's range and panic sells the bottom of it. Both are the market memory model working against you instead of for you.

5

Wait for the John Wick candle

  • A sharp wick through a remembered level, followed by a distinct close back on the other side.
  • It works in both directions — a wick above resistance that closes red, or a wick below support that closes green.
  • It's the moment the level gets rejected live, in front of you, instead of you guessing it will.

Mid-candle, a real rejection and a real breakout look identical. The John Wick candle is the close that settles the argument.

6

Entry, stop, target

  • Enter on the break of the John Wick candle in the direction of the rejection.
  • Stop goes on the far side of that same candle — above the high for a short, below the low for a long.
  • Target is the other side of the range. This is an auction between two remembered extremes, not a prediction of a new one.
  • Move the stop to breakeven once price clears the level with a real hold.

The stop lives on the candle that proved the level, not on the level itself — size off the trigger, never off the number you were only watching.

7

The backside trade

  • When price keeps pressing into a level and keeps failing, it's building a small range on the wrong side of the bigger one.
  • Once that small range breaks toward the remembered side, the participants who forced the failed pushes go into panic exit.
  • Same model, later in the day — let the smaller range fail, then ride it back toward the other extreme.

A level defended twice in one session doesn't quietly disappear by lunchtime. Fading the exhaustion here is trusting the same memory, just later in the day.

Same model, three different times of day

These feel like different setups. They're one setup, and the trigger never changes.

At the open

Price tests yesterday's high or low straight out of the gate. This is where the two mistakes — buying sellers, selling buyers — happen fastest.

Midday, narrowing range

New micro-levels form and get tested again and again, pulling in less each time. Same rules, smaller box.

Backside, after 12:00

Repeated failed pushes build a small range on the wrong side of the bigger one, then it breaks toward the remembered extreme.

The four filters

These don't find the trade — the John Wick candle does that. They exist to keep you off the setups that only look like this one.

1  The catalyst

Yesterday needs a real reason behind the move: news, earnings, or a clean technical break or failure of a key range.

Kills: random names with no memory to trade.

2  The clean-lines filter

Three to four levels, maximum. If it takes more than that to describe the day, the structure isn't clear enough to trust.

Kills: over-drawn charts and manufactured setups.

3  The hold filter

A poke past a level doesn't count on its own. It has to hold, or fail back, with a real close — not a wick you caught mid-candle.

Kills: chasing before the candle settles.

4  The middle filter

No trade with no level nearby. If price is drifting through open air between the lines, there's nothing to react to yet.

Kills: guessing inside the range.

Size off the wick, never off the level

The level and the top of the wick can sit well apart — the market overshoots the line before it rejects. Your risk is measured from where the candle actually proved the level, not from the line itself.

Risk budget for the trade$500.00
Entry — break of the John Wick candle's low76.40
Stop — high of that candle78.15
Risk per share$1.75
Position size285 shares

The mistake this prevents

Size off the remembered level instead and you measure risk as 77.00 − 76.40 = $0.60, which says sell short 833 shares.

833 × $1.75 = $1,458 at your actual stop.

Nearly three times the risk you budgeted, on a stop the trade hadn't even reached yet.

The trade is the reaction. The level just tells you where to expect one.

The story changes every day. The lines don't. That's the reason to run this mechanically instead of freelancing a new read every morning.

  • Trading a level with no real prior-day decision behind it isn't memory, it's a guess.
  • The John Wick candle is the confirmation, not the setup. Waiting for the close costs a few cents and saves you the trades that go nowhere.
  • If price closes back through the level after you're in, the memory just changed. Leave — don't wait for the stop to tell you.
