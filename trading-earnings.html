When the call kills the trade

1 · The headline reaction: watch it, don't chase it

When the report comes out, the first question isn't whether the company beat estimates. It's how the market reacts to the numbers. The stock doesn't care whether the headline sounds good. It cares whether the results are better or worse than what investors were already expecting.

The beat that faded in five minutes

1

What happened with SpaceX

  • The earnings headline came out and the numbers looked good.
  • The stock got above 150 and spiked toward 152.
  • Then it faded right back. Anyone who bought that first spike was immediately in a difficult position.

The best long came later, after this move failed, from information that wasn't in the headline.

2

What to watch instead of buying

  • Where the stock settles after the first move.
  • Whether it starts holding a level.
  • Whether the selling slows down, and whether buyers come back.

And meanwhile, wait for the next source of information: the conference call.

Why would good news fade?

You don't need to know which one it is. What matters is that buyers didn't hold the move.

2 · The conference call: what actually changes the story

This is the part many short-term traders underestimate. They see the release and the first reaction and think the event is over. But the headline is a snapshot of what the company just did. The call tells you what might happen next, and sometimes it completely changes the direction of the stock.

Five things to listen for

1 · story

Contracts

Is the company announcing new business? Are customers actually committing to spend, and is it big relative to the company's size?

"Demand is strong" is not the same as "we've signed contracts that support future revenue."
2 · story

Next quarter's guidance

One of the biggest things to focus on. A great quarter with disappointing guidance can get crushed. An okay quarter with raised expectations can move a lot higher.

SpaceX's revenue guidance gave the market a reason to rethink the weak first reaction.
3 · supply

Financing

For companies spending enormous amounts to grow: can cash flow fund it? Does it need to borrow? Does it need to issue stock?

A huge growth opportunity can still come with financing challenges.
4 · supply

Dilution

If the company raises money by selling more shares, existing shareholders have to weigh the impact, and the market has to absorb the new supply.

A bullish growth story doesn't automatically mean the stock goes up tomorrow.
5 · supply

Share unlocks

Are large blocks of shares becoming eligible for sale? How much stock could enter the market?

Not everyone will sell, but buyers may have to absorb the extra supply.
1

What the SpaceX call said

  • The tone was very bullish.
  • Management discussed significant capital expenditures, and importantly tied that spending to contracts.
  • They gave very strong revenue guidance for the next quarter.

Now the conversation isn't about whether the company beat last quarter. It's about how much revenue the business could generate going forward.

You're not becoming an expert in 30 minutes

You don't sit through the call analyzing every sentence. You're trying to spot the information that could materially change expectations, and then see whether the market agrees. The SpaceX call was bullish enough to start looking for a long. One thing was still missing: price action confirming the idea.

3 · Price confirmation: where the trade actually happens

This is the difference between having an opinion about earnings and trading earnings. You can listen to a great call, understand why the company might be worth more, and still lose money buying the stock at the wrong price. So the information always gets connected to a specific level on the chart.

From opinion to trade

1

Why long at 154 and not at 152

  • The initial headline move had already failed.
  • There was new information from the call.
  • The stock was now showing buying interest at higher prices.

That combination was much more compelling than the original spike toward 152. Buying exactly 154 isn't the point; the reasons are.

2

Use prior levels to define your risk

  • Before the trade, know where the stock found support or resistance before.
  • Reclaiming an important level? Can it hold above it?
  • Breaking resistance? Does it keep attracting buyers, or fall right back into the old range?

You don't need to predict the next $10 move. You need the price where your read is no longer being confirmed.

4 · Plan A and Plan B for the next day

Even after a bullish call there can be very real reasons for the stock to struggle. Those competing forces are why you build two plans instead of walking in with one prediction: what you do if buyers take control, and what you do if sellers do.

Hold 155–156, or lose it

Plan A · the bullish case

Buyers defend 155–156

  • The call was strong: contracts behind the capital spending, very bullish next-quarter revenue guidance.
  • After the headline reaction failed, the stock found buyers and moved higher. Are they still there today?
  • If it holds 155–156 despite selling pressure, buyers are still willing to own it at these prices.
  • If it keeps building higher, a close above 160 becomes a reasonable upside scenario. Not a promise.

Holds early, moves higher, buyers keep defending the area: interested in the long side.

Plan B · the supply problem

It loses 155–156 and can't reclaim it

  • Financing: spending on the order of $500 billion on infrastructure next year. Borrow? Sell stock? At what cost?
  • Unlock: roughly 900 million shares becoming eligible for sale the next day. That's possible new supply, not a promise anyone sells.
  • Excited buyers and shareholders who want out, plus traders selling ahead of them, can all be there at once.
  • If it keeps testing 155–156 and can't hold, stop forcing the long.

Holding below the levels that supported the post-call move: the supply may be winning over the bullish story.

Same process, different direction

The call can strengthen a weak first reaction, like SpaceX. It can also undermine a strong one. Plenty of Tesla earnings calls have opened with a positive reaction, then management talked about something investors didn't like (margins, spending, guidance) and the stock started selling off.

The call can change the trade

The four-step earnings checklist

The whole process in order. Click through it before your next earnings trade.

1

Watch the headline reaction

  • Does the initial move hold or fail?
  • Don't chase it just because the headline sounds good.
  • Watch where it settles, whether selling slows, and whether buyers come back.

The stock reacts to expectations, not to the headline.

2

Listen to the conference call

  • Contracts, next-quarter guidance, financing, dilution and share unlocks.
  • Look for information that changes the market's expectations.
  • Then see whether the market agrees.

The headline is what the company did. The call is what might happen next.

3

Wait for price confirmation

  • Where are the important levels?
  • Are buyers defending them?
  • Is the stock reclaiming resistance or failing at it?

That's where you can build a trade with defined risk.

4

Build Plan A and Plan B

  • What would confirm the bullish case for tomorrow?
  • What would invalidate it?
  • What new information might explain why the stock moves one way rather than the other?

Write both down before the open. Then let the stock tell you which one it is.

Headline, then the call, then price.

The headline tells you what happened. The call tells you what might happen next. Price tells you whether the market agrees. Separate the fundamental story from the actual trade, and never decide what an earnings trade should do before you've heard the important information and watched the stock respond.

  • Watch the first spike. Don't chase it.
  • A faded beat isn't a short. It's unconfirmed.
  • Listen for contracts, guidance, financing, dilution and unlocks.
  • Connect the information to a level. Buy acceptance, not a headline.
  • Know the price where your read stops being confirmed.
  • Write Plan A and Plan B before the next open.
