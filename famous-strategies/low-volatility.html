Low-Volatility Anomaly

Shouldn't riskier stocks pay more? In the data, the calmest stocks earned about as much as the wildest, and the noisiest did worst of all. Own the calm ones, rebalance on a schedule, and accept lagging in roaring rallies. Less risk, not less return.

What is the low-volatility anomaly?

The low-volatility anomaly is the finding that the calmest stocks earned about as much as the riskiest, and the noisiest did worst, against the textbook idea that more risk pays more. It comes from Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang (2006) and Frazzini and Pedersen's Betting Against Beta (2014). The strategy owns the calm stocks and rebalances on a schedule.

The line that should slope up

Every finance course draws the same picture: more risk, more expected return. The low-volatility anomaly is what happens when you check that picture against the data.

Flat, not rising

1

The rule

  • Measure each stock's risk over the past year: its volatility (how much it swings) or its beta (how much it moves with the market).
  • Rank the list from calmest to wildest, and own the calmest group, spread across many names and sectors.
  • Re-rank on a fixed schedule, monthly or quarterly, and trade as little as possible in between.

The long-short research version also shorts the wildest stocks and uses leverage to make the two sides match in beta. A single account usually just owns the calm side.

Volatility or beta?

Two ways to measure "calm", and they mostly agree.

volatilityHow much it swings

The standard deviation of returns. Easy to compute for any stock or list.

betaHow much it moves with the market

The papers' measure for betting against beta. High-beta stocks amplify the market's moves.

The noisiest stocks did worst

Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang looked at a different kind of risk: the swings that belong to the company alone, not to the market. The stocks with the most of it had strikingly bad returns.

Idiosyncratic volatility

2

What the 2006 paper found

  • Idiosyncratic volatility: the daily swings left over after the market, size and value factors, measured over the past month.
  • Sorted into five groups and held for a month, the noisiest fifth earned 1.06% a month less than the calmest. After the Fama-French factors, 1.31% less.
  • Exposure to market-wide volatility, which they also studied, didn't explain it.

If high volatility were a risk investors had to be paid for, the noisiest stocks would earn more. They earned less.

From the abstract: "stocks with high idiosyncratic volatility relative to the Fama and French (1993) model have abysmally low average returns."

Betting against beta

Frazzini and Pedersen turned the pattern into a market-neutral factor: lever up the calm stocks, scale down the wild ones, and hold both so the market cancels out.

Why it lasts, and what it costs

An anomaly this old needs a reason to survive. The research gives three, and every one of them is about how investors are constrained or judged, not about a mistake that will be fixed tomorrow.

Three reasons, and the price

  1. 1972Black, Jensen & Scholes

    Early tests find the line between beta and return flatter than the theory says.

  2. 2006Ang, Hodrick, Xing & Zhang

    The noisiest stocks have "abysmally low" returns.

  3. 2011Baker, Bradley & Wurgler

    Benchmarked fund managers have reasons to avoid low-beta stocks.

  4. 2014Frazzini & Pedersen

    Betting against beta works in US stocks since 1926, and in other markets.

  5. SinceLow-vol funds

    Low-volatility and minimum-variance ETFs make the tilt easy to own, and more crowded.

The strategy, step by step

The whole thing in five steps. Click through them.

1

Measure

  • Each stock's volatility of daily returns over the past year, or its beta to the market.
  • Use the same window for every stock.

Past risk is a good guide to future risk, much better than past returns are to future returns.

2

Rank

  • Sort the list from calmest to wildest.
  • Bigger lists give the effect more room to show up.

It's a ranking, like momentum and value.

3

Hold

  • Own the calmest group, a third or a fifth of the list.
  • Spread it over many names; calm stocks bunch in a few sectors.

Watch for too much in utilities and staples at once.

4

Rebalance

  • Re-rank monthly or quarterly.
  • Trade only the names that move out of the group.

Low turnover keeps costs down.

5

Expect

  • It will lag in strong rallies, sometimes for years.
  • It should lose less in falls.

The lagging is why the edge is still there.

We tested it Lower risk, lower return

Smaller drops, smaller gains

The calmest 20% of stocks did what the papers say on risk: the worst drop was only 10%. But they made well under the average stock.

  • +7.7%a year, calmest 20%
  • −10%worst drop
  • +12.9%equal-weight market

How we tested. Every US stock that traded from Nov 2021 to Nov 2026, delisted ones included, so there's no survivorship bias. Company figures from SEC filings, used only from the day they were filed. 0.5 bp a side on SPY, QQQ and ETFs, 5 bp a side on single stocks. Stock returns are price only; ETF returns include dividends. Five years is short for a monthly strategy, and 2022 to 2026 was a strong large-cap market that is hard for any timing rule to beat. +1R means a trade won what it risked. The indicator

One TradingView script for this strategy (Pine Script v6). It ranks the chart's stock against a list of up to 19 others on volatility over the past year, shows its beta to SPY, and alerts when it moves into or out of the calmest group. It marks and alerts. It doesn't place trades.

  • Low-Volatility Rank

    alerts

    A pane under your chart with the stock's annualized volatility (gold) against the median of your list (gray). The background turns green while the stock ranks among the calmest N of the list.

    A table ranks the whole list from calmest to wildest: the calmest N in green, the wildest N in red, the chart's stock highlighted, and its beta to the benchmark at the bottom. It ships with 19 large US stocks, from utilities to chip makers.

    Volatility is the standard deviation of daily log returns over the lookback, annualized. Every number is a setting: the lookback, how many count as calm, the benchmark and the list.

//@version=6
// =====================================================================
// Low-Volatility Rank — thetrademethod.com
// https://thetrademethod.com/famous-strategies/low-volatility.html
//
// Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang (2006) and Frazzini and Pedersen (2014):
// calmer, lower-beta stocks have earned about as much as wild ones, and
// far more for the risk taken. The most volatile stocks have done worst.
//
//   1. Volatility  Each stock's annualized volatility of daily returns
//                  over the lookback (252 days by default).
//   2. Rank        The chart's stock against a list you give it (up to
//                  19 others), calmest first.
//   3. Beta        The chart's stock's beta to the benchmark (SPY), over
//                  the same window.
//
// The background turns green while the stock is among the calmest N of
// the list. Use a daily chart. Marks and alerts only; no orders.
// =====================================================================
indicator("Low-Volatility Rank", shorttitle = "Low Vol", overlay = false)

grpS = "Settings"
len     = input.int(252, "Lookback (trading days)", minval = 20, maxval = 756, group = grpS)
topN    = input.int(5, "Count as calmest", minval = 1, maxval = 20, group = grpS)
bench   = input.symbol("AMEX:SPY", "Benchmark for beta", group = grpS)
showTbl = input.bool(true, "Show the table", group = grpS)
grpL = "The list to rank"
s1  = input.symbol("NYSE:KO", "1", group = grpL)
s2  = input.symbol("NYSE:PG", "2", group = grpL)
s3  = input.symbol("NYSE:JNJ", "3", group = grpL)
s4  = input.symbol("NASDAQ:PEP", "4", group = grpL)
s5  = input.symbol("NYSE:WMT", "5", group = grpL)
s6  = input.symbol("NYSE:MCD", "6", group = grpL)
s7  = input.symbol("NYSE:DUK", "7", group = grpL)
s8  = input.symbol("NYSE:SO", "8", group = grpL)
s9  = input.symbol("NYSE:VZ", "9", group = grpL)
s10 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:COST", "10", group = grpL)
s11 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:AAPL", "11", group = grpL)
s12 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:MSFT", "12", group = grpL)
s13 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:NVDA", "13", group = grpL)
s14 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:TSLA", "14", group = grpL)
s15 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:AMD", "15", group = grpL)
s16 = input.symbol("NYSE:CCL", "16", group = grpL)
s17 = input.symbol("NASDAQ:MRNA", "17", group = grpL)
s18 = input.symbol("NYSE:XOM", "18", group = grpL)
s19 = input.symbol("NYSE:JPM", "19", group = grpL)

volOf(string sym) =>
    request.security(sym, "D", ta.stdev(math.log(close / close[1]), len) * math.sqrt(252) * 100, ignore_invalid_symbol = true)

syms = array.from(syminfo.tickerid, s1, s2, s3, s4, s5, s6, s7, s8, s9, s10, s11, s12, s13, s14, s15, s16, s17, s18, s19)
n = array.size(syms)
vols = array.new<float>(n, na)
for i = 0 to n - 1
    array.set(vols, i, volOf(array.get(syms, i)))

rankOf(int i) =>
    v = array.get(vols, i)
    rk = 1
    for j = 0 to n - 1
        w = array.get(vols, j)
        if j != i and not na(w) and (na(v) or w < v)
            rk += 1
    rk

myVol  = array.get(vols, 0)
myRank = rankOf(0)
calm   = not na(myVol) and myRank <= topN

// beta of the chart's stock to the benchmark (daily returns)
r  = math.log(close / close[1])
bc = request.security(bench, timeframe.period, close, ignore_invalid_symbol = true)
rb = math.log(bc / bc[1])
beta = ta.correlation(r, rb, len) * ta.stdev(r, len) / ta.stdev(rb, len)

// median volatility of the list, for the reference line
med = array.median(vols)

plot(myVol, "Volatility % (annualized)", color = #f4b942, linewidth = 2)
plot(med, "List median", color = color.new(#8e8e93, 20), linewidth = 1)
bgcolor(calm ? color.new(#30d158, 90) : na, title = "Among the calmest")
alertcondition(calm and not calm[1], "Moved into the calmest", "{{ticker}} is now among the calmest stocks on your list")
alertcondition(not calm and calm[1], "Left the calmest", "{{ticker}} is no longer among the calmest stocks on your list")

var table tb = table.new(position.top_right, 3, 22, bgcolor = color.new(#1d1d1f, 0), border_width = 1, border_color = color.new(#2c2c2e, 0))
if showTbl and barstate.islast
    lc = color.new(#86868b, 0)
    vc = color.new(#f5f5f7, 0)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 0, "#", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 0, "Symbol", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 2, 0, "Vol %", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    row = 1
    for p = 1 to n
        for i = 0 to n - 1
            if rankOf(i) == p and row <= n
                v = array.get(vols, i)
                bg = i == 0 ? color.new(#5b4a1f, 0) : p <= topN ? color.new(#173d24, 0) : p > n - topN ? color.new(#4a1d1b, 0) : color.new(#1d1d1f, 0)
                table.cell(tb, 0, row, str.tostring(p), text_color = lc, bgcolor = bg, text_size = size.tiny)
                table.cell(tb, 1, row, i == 0 ? syminfo.ticker + " (chart)" : array.get(syms, i), text_color = vc, bgcolor = bg, text_size = size.tiny)
                table.cell(tb, 2, row, na(v) ? "n/a" : str.tostring(v, "#"), text_color = vc, bgcolor = bg, text_size = size.tiny)
                row += 1
    table.cell(tb, 0, row, "β", text_color = lc, text_size = size.tiny)
    table.cell(tb, 1, row, "beta to " + bench, text_color = lc, text_size = size.tiny)
    table.cell(tb, 2, row, na(beta) ? "n/a" : str.tostring(beta, "#.00"), text_color = vc, text_size = size.tiny)

Questions

Volatility or beta?

Two ways to measure calm. Volatility is how much a stock swings; beta is how much it moves with the market. They mostly agree.

How big was the effect in the papers?

The noisiest fifth of stocks trailed the calmest by 1.06% a month. Lowest-beta stocks had a 0.70 Sharpe ratio against 0.28 for the highest, and betting against beta had a 0.78 Sharpe from 1926 to 2012.

What is the catch?

It lags in roaring rallies.

Does low volatility work?

Lower risk, lower return in our test. The calmest 20% had a worst drop of only 10% but made +7.7% a year against +12.9% for the average stock.

Try it on paper first.

Take 30 large stocks, rank them on one-year volatility, and write down the calmest ten. Track them against the S&P 500 for a year, re-ranking each quarter. Note how they did in the months the market fell, and the months it ripped.

  • Fix the list, the window and the rebalance dates first.
  • Check sector weights each quarter.
  • Write down what you'll do if it lags a hot market for a year.
