Cross-Sectional Momentum

The idea in one picture

Most trading looks at one chart at a time. Cross-sectional momentum looks at every stock at once and asks a single question: which ones have gone up the most, and which have gone down the most, compared with each other?

Rank everything, trade the ends

1

The rule

  • At the start of each month, rank every stock in the universe on its return over the past J months (3, 6, 9 or 12 in the paper).
  • Buy the top tenth (the winners) and sell short the bottom tenth (the losers), with every stock in each group getting the same weight.
  • Hold for K months (3, 6, 9 or 12), then the position rolls off. A fresh portfolio is built every month.

There's no chart reading, no stop and no target. The ranking does all the work, which is what makes it testable over decades of data.

Cross-sectional is not the same as trend

Two kinds of momentum get mixed up all the time.

cross-sectionalBetter than the others?

Each stock is ranked against every other stock on the same date. In a crash, the "winners" can be the stocks that fell the least. This page.

time-seriesBetter than its own past?

Each asset is judged on its own: own it if its own past return is positive. That's trend following (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012), and the idea behind the 10-month moving average and Dual Momentum pages.

Same idea, older name. The paper calls these "relative strength strategies": the same relative strength traders were screening for long before 1993, tested here on every NYSE and AMEX stock over 25 years.

The clock: look back, skip, hold

Every version of the strategy is two numbers. J is how far back you measure. K is how long you hold. The paper tested every pair of 3, 6, 9 and 12 months, and ran each one again with a one-week gap before buying.

J months back, K months held

2

Overlap smooths the timing luck

  • With a 6-month hold, you don't rebuild the whole book every six months. Each month you build a new portfolio and retire the one from six months ago.
  • So you're always holding six slices, each formed in a different month. If one ranking date was unlucky, it's only a sixth of the money.
  • The paper's portfolios work exactly this way: in any month the strategy holds the portfolios picked this month and in the previous K−1 months.

For a single account, a monthly full rebalance is the simpler version. It trades more, but you only have one list to manage.

Why skip the most recent stretch?

Over very short windows, the pattern flips. Last week's or last month's biggest movers tend to snap back a little, and closing prices bounce between the bid and the ask. Ranking on a window that ends right now mixes that reversal into the signal.

Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993Skip one week

Every test was run twice: buying right away, and buying one week after ranking.

later researchSkip one month

The common convention since: rank on the return from 12 months ago to 1 month ago ("12–2" or "12−1").

What the paper found

All sixteen combinations made money in the 1965–1989 sample. The interesting part is what happens if you don't let go: the paper also followed the portfolio for three years after it was formed.

A year of drift, then a fade

3

The numbers

  • Best version: rank on 12 months, hold 3. It made 1.31% a month, or 1.49% a month with the one-week skip.
  • The version the paper studies most, 6 months by 6 months, made a compounded excess return of 12.01% a year.
  • Not a risk story: the winners portfolio didn't have a higher beta than the losers (1.28 against 1.36), so winners minus losers was close to market-neutral.

The paper's reading: prices underreact to company news at first, so the move keeps going for months, then overshoot, which is why part of it comes back in years two and three.

The January problem. In the paper, the strategy lost about 7% on average every January, when beaten-down losers bounce. Outside January it did better than its overall average. If you run it, expect January to hurt.

When it breaks: the momentum crash

Momentum's long-run record comes with rare, violent losses. They don't happen in a falling market. They happen when a falling market suddenly turns, and the short side is full of the stocks that bounce hardest.

Spring 2009: the losers rip

4

Why the short side blows up

  • After a big decline, the loser decile is packed with the stocks that fell hardest, which are usually the highest-beta names.
  • When the market rebounds, those names rebound the most. Being short them is like being short the market with leverage.
  • It isn't new: Daniel and Moskowitz found the same pattern in 1932, when winners minus losers lost about 61% in July and 74% in August.

This is why the long-only, top-few version on this page is popular with individual traders: it skips the short book that does the damage in a crash, at the cost of carrying market risk.

Two published fixes

Both scale the position down when the risk is highest, instead of trying to predict the turn.

Barroso & Santa-Clara, 2015Volatility scaling

Size the momentum portfolio by its own recent volatility: smaller when it's been swinging hard. It improved the risk-adjusted return and largely sidestepped the crashes in their sample.

Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016Dynamic weighting

Cut exposure in "panic states": after market declines and when volatility is high. Their dynamic version roughly doubled the Sharpe ratio of the static strategy.

A version a single account can run

The paper's version holds hundreds of stocks long and short. Most traders run something smaller: rank a list, hold the top few, re-rank every month. Here's how that looks month to month.

Hold the top 3, re-rank monthly

rule one

Rank, don't pick

The list decides what you own. If you override the ranking because you don't like a name, you're no longer running the strategy you tested.

rule two

Skip the last month

Rank on the return from 12 months ago to 1 month ago. Last month's biggest movers tend to snap back.

rule three

Count the costs

Momentum trades a lot. Spreads, commissions and short-term taxes come straight out of the edge, so rebalance on a fixed schedule, not every time the list shifts.

rule four

Respect the crash

After a big market decline, when volatility is high, run smaller. That's when momentum has historically given back years of gains in a couple of months.

It didn't go away after it was published

Plenty of anomalies vanish once they're in a journal. Momentum is one of the most tested results in finance, and it kept showing up, out of sample and in other markets.

  1. 1993Jegadeesh & Titman

  2. 1997Carhart

    Adds a one-year momentum factor to the Fama-French model to explain mutual fund returns: the four-factor model.

  3. 1998Rouwenhorst

    The same effect in 12 European stock markets.

  4. 2001Jegadeesh & Titman

    Out of sample: momentum kept working through the 1990s, after the original paper.

  5. 2013Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen

    "Value and Momentum Everywhere": momentum in stocks across countries, and in other asset classes too.

  6. 2016Daniel & Moskowitz

    "Momentum Crashes": the rare, sharp losses, when they happen and how to scale around them.

The strategy, step by step

The whole thing in five steps. Click through them.

1

Pick the universe

  • The paper used every NYSE and AMEX stock in its database. For one account, a broad, liquid list works: the S&P 500, or US stocks above a price and volume floor.
  • Decide it once and keep it fixed. Changing the list changes the strategy.

Thin, cheap stocks often land at the extremes of the ranking, and they're the ones you can't trade at the price on the screen.

2

Measure past return

  • For every stock, take the total return over the lookback: 3 to 12 months in the paper, most often 12 months today.
  • Leave out the most recent stretch: one week in the paper, one month in most later research (months t−12 to t−2).

The skip keeps last month's short-term reversal out of a signal that's meant to last months.

3

Rank into tenths

  • Sort from best to worst and cut the list into ten equal groups.
  • The top tenth are the winners, the bottom tenth are the losers. The middle eight tenths aren't traded.

It's all relative: in a bear market, the winners can be stocks that are simply down less than everything else.

4

Long winners, short losers

  • Buy the winners and short the losers, equal dollars on each side and equal weight in each stock.
  • Long-only variant: hold just the top names, and accept that you now carry the market's ups and downs.

The paper's return is winners minus losers. The short side is what makes it market-neutral, and what hurts in a rebound.

5

Hold, then re-rank

  • Hold for the K months you chose (3 to 12 in the paper), then rank again and rebuild.
  • Overlapping monthly slices, or one full monthly rebalance: pick one and stick to the calendar.

The edge was strongest in the first year, and more than half of the first year's gain faded over the next two. Don't marry the winners.

Try it on paper first.

Take the S&P 500, rank it on the return from 12 months ago to 1 month ago, and write down the top ten on the first trading day of the month. Do it again next month, and the month after. Track the list against the index for a year before you put a dollar on it, and note every January.

  • Fix the universe, the lookback, the skip and the rebalance day before you start.
  • Count every swap, so you can see what the turnover costs.
  • Write down what you'll do after a big market decline, before you're in one.
