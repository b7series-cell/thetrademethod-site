Post-Earnings Drift

Does a stock that blows out its earnings keep going after the first day? Score every company's surprise against its own history, buy the biggest beats, short the biggest misses, and hold for about a quarter. The news takes weeks to sink in.

What is post-earnings announcement drift?

Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) is the tendency of stocks with big earnings surprises to keep moving the same way for weeks. Bernard and Thomas (1989) scored each surprise against the company's own history, bought the biggest beats, shorted the biggest misses and held for about 60 days.

The idea in one picture

Every quarter a company reports earnings, and the market already has a number in mind. The strategy only cares about the gap between the two, and how unusual that gap is for this particular company.

Scoring the surprise

1

The rule

  • When a company reports, measure its surprise: reported earnings minus what was expected.
  • Divide by how much this company's earnings usually miss the forecast. That's the standardized unexpected earnings, or SUE.
  • Each quarter, sort every company that reported into ten groups by SUE. Buy the top group (biggest beats), sell short the bottom group (biggest misses), and hold for about a quarter.

There's no chart reading in the original. The surprise picks the stocks and the calendar picks the exit, which is why it could be tested over thousands of reports.

Two ways to measure "expected"

The papers and the traders don't use the same forecast.

the paperLast year, plus growth

Bernard and Thomas forecast each quarter from the same quarter a year earlier, plus the company's usual growth. No analysts needed.

todayThe analysts' consensus

Most data feeds and screeners, and the indicator below, compare reported EPS with the consensus estimate instead.

The drift

If prices took in earnings news at once, the line after a report would go flat. Bernard and Thomas lined up thousands of reports and found it doesn't: the good news keeps rising and the bad news keeps falling, for weeks.

60 days after the report

2

What the paper found

  • The long-short position (top tenth of SUE minus bottom tenth) made money in 41 of 48 quarters from 1974 to 1985.
  • It was positive in 11 of the 16 quarters when the NYSE index fell, so it wasn't just riding the market.
  • The drift was larger in smaller companies, and a big share of it arrived around the next three earnings reports.

The paper's title asks the real question: is the drift a reward for some hidden risk, or are prices just slow? Their evidence pointed to slow prices.

Older than momentum. Ball and Brown saw it first, in 1968: returns kept moving in the direction of the earnings news after the report. Bernard and Thomas turned it into a strategy and tried to explain it away with risk. They couldn't.

Delayed reaction or risk?

The two explanations the paper tested.

risk premiumYou're paid for risk

If the drift were pay for extra risk, the long-short would lose in bad markets. It made money in 11 of 16 down quarters.

delayed responsePrices are slow

Investors under-react to the news and catch up over the next weeks and reports. The paper's evidence favored this.

Why it jumps at the next report

Bernard and Thomas's 1990 follow-up asked where the drift happens. A surprising amount of it lands in a few days: right around the company's next earnings reports.

Jumps at the next reports

3

Earnings surprises repeat

  • A beat this quarter tends to be followed by smaller beats in the same direction over the next three quarters, then a partial reversal a year out.
  • Prices act as if investors expect next quarter to look like the same quarter last year, missing that the surprise will partly repeat.
  • So when the next report confirms it, the stock jumps again. About 25–30% of the drift fell in the 3-day windows around the next reports, roughly 5% of the trading days.

That's why the hold runs through the next report. Exit just before it and you skip one of the biggest pieces.

It has faded

Post-earnings drift is one of the most studied anomalies in finance. That's a problem for anyone hoping to trade the plain version today.

A famous anomaly gets smaller

  1. 1968Ball & Brown

    Returns keep moving in the direction of the earnings news after the report: the first sighting.

  2. 1989Bernard & Thomas

    The long-short SUE strategy, positive in 41 of 48 quarters, and not explained by risk.

  3. 1990Bernard & Thomas

    The drift bunches up around the next reports, because surprises repeat and prices don't expect it.

  4. 2022Kettell, McInnis & Zhao

    The top-minus-bottom SUE spread falls from about 5% to 3% or less, partly because surprises repeat less than they used to.

A version a single account can run

The papers trade every company at once. Traders usually take one name at a time: a big surprise, with price agreeing with the news, held for weeks rather than minutes.

One stock, one report

rule one

Only big surprises

Small beats and misses are noise. Set the size that counts, as a score or a percent, before the report.

rule two

Price has to agree

A beat that gaps up and holds is the setup. A beat the stock sells off is the market telling you something else.

rule three

Hold through the next report

A big piece of the drift arrives at the next earnings date. Decide in advance whether you're holding through it.

rule four

Expect less than the paper

The spread has roughly halved since the 1990s. Count costs, and size it like an edge that's smaller than it used to be.

The strategy, step by step

The whole thing in five steps. Click through them.

1

Know the forecast

  • Before the report, know what was expected: the consensus estimate, or last year's same quarter plus growth.
  • Write down what size of surprise you'll act on.

The plan comes before the number.

2

Score the surprise

  • Surprise = reported minus expected.
  • Divide by how much this company usually misses. A score of 1 or more is a big surprise for this company.

Eight cents is huge for a steady utility and nothing for a chip maker.

3

Sort

  • In the paper: every company that reported this quarter, sorted into ten groups by score.
  • For one account: only the reports in the top or bottom of your list.

The edge is in the extremes, not the middle.

4

Trade

  • Buy the biggest beats. The paper also shorts the biggest misses.
  • Traders often wait for price to confirm: a gap that holds into the close.

Long-only is simpler, and it's what most accounts run.

5

Hold

  • About a quarter (60 trading days) in the research, through the next report.
  • Or set a stop under the report-day low and trail it under higher lows.

The drift is weeks long. Don't take it off in the first hour.

We tested it Weak

A weak drift, positive about half the time

The biggest earnings surprises beat the biggest misses by about 1% over the next 60 days, and that held in only 11 of 20 quarters.

  • +1.0%per 60 days, top minus bottom tenth
  • 11 of 20quarters positive
  • The surprise was measured from the 10-Q filing date, which comes after the press release, so some of the drift had already happened. Consensus estimates weren't available.

How we tested. Every US stock that traded from Nov 2021 to Nov 2026, delisted ones included, so there's no survivorship bias. Company figures from SEC filings, used only from the day they were filed. 0.5 bp a side on SPY, QQQ and ETFs, 5 bp a side on single stocks. Stock returns are price only; ETF returns include dividends. Five years is short for a monthly strategy, and 2022 to 2026 was a strong large-cap market that is hard for any timing rule to beat. +1R means a trade won what it risked. The indicator

One TradingView script for this strategy (Pine Script v6). It reads TradingView's own earnings data, scores each surprise against the company's recent history, shades the drift window that follows a big beat or miss, and tracks the stock against SPY since the report. It marks and alerts. It doesn't place trades.

    alerts

    A label on every report: beat or miss, the surprise as a percent of the estimate, and a SUE-style score (this surprise divided by the spread of the last 8 surprises). A big beat shades the next 60 trading days green, a big miss red.

    A table on the right tracks the latest report: the surprise, the score, days since, the stock's move since the day before the report, and how far ahead of or behind the benchmark (SPY by default) it is.

    It uses the consensus estimate, not the paper's seasonal forecast. Every number is a setting: how many surprises feed the score, what counts as big, the window length and the benchmark. Alerts fire on a big beat or a big miss.

//@version=6
// =====================================================================
// Post-Earnings Drift — thetrademethod.com
// https://thetrademethod.com/famous-strategies/post-earnings-drift.html
//
// Bernard and Thomas (1989, 1990): after an earnings surprise, prices keep
// drifting in the surprise's direction for weeks, and a big share of the
// drift arrives around the NEXT few earnings reports.
//
//   1. Surprise   Reported EPS against the consensus estimate, as a percent.
//   2. Score      A SUE-style score: this quarter's surprise divided by the
//                 standard deviation of the last N surprises, so a "beat"
//                 is judged against how noisy this company usually is.
//   3. Drift      The window after each report (default 60 trading days)
//                 is shaded green after a big beat, red after a big miss.
//   4. Track      The table shows the stock's return since the report
//                 against the benchmark's (SPY by default): the drift so far.
//
// Use a daily chart on a stock with earnings data. Marks and alerts only;
// it places no orders.
// =====================================================================
indicator("Post-Earnings Drift", shorttitle = "PEAD", overlay = true, max_labels_count = 200)

// ---------------- inputs ----------------
grpS = "Surprise"
nHist    = input.int(8, "Surprises used for the score", minval = 4, maxval = 20, group = grpS)
sueBig   = input.float(1.0, "Big surprise (score at least)", minval = 0.1, step = 0.1, group = grpS)
pctBig   = input.float(5.0, "...or a surprise of at least (%)", minval = 0, step = 0.5, group = grpS, tooltip = "Used until there are enough past surprises to compute the score.")
grpD = "Drift"
driftLen = input.int(60, "Drift window (trading days)", minval = 5, maxval = 250, group = grpD)
bench    = input.symbol("AMEX:SPY", "Benchmark", group = grpD)
showTbl  = input.bool(true, "Show the table", group = grpD)

// ---------------- earnings ----------------
act = request.earnings(syminfo.tickerid, earnings.actual, barmerge.gaps_on, ignore_invalid_symbol = true)
est = request.earnings(syminfo.tickerid, earnings.estimate, barmerge.gaps_on, ignore_invalid_symbol = true)
bClose = request.security(bench, timeframe.period, close, ignore_invalid_symbol = true)

var hist = array.new<float>()
var int   eBar  = na
var float eSurp = na
var float eSue  = na
var float base  = na
var float bBase = na
var int   eDir  = 0

isNew = not na(act) and not na(est)
if isNew
    diff = act - est
    sd = array.size(hist) >= 4 ? array.stdev(hist) : na
    eSue  := not na(sd) and sd > 0 ? diff / sd : na
    eSurp := est != 0 ? diff / math.abs(est) * 100 : na
    eBar  := bar_index
    base  := close[1]
    bBase := bClose[1]
    big = not na(eSue) ? math.abs(eSue) >= sueBig : not na(eSurp) and math.abs(eSurp) >= pctBig
    eDir  := big ? (diff > 0 ? 1 : -1) : 0
    array.push(hist, diff)
    if array.size(hist) > nHist
        array.shift(hist)
    txtL = (diff >= 0 ? "Beat " : "Miss ") + (na(eSurp) ? "" : (eSurp >= 0 ? "+" : "") + str.tostring(eSurp, "#.0") + "%") + (na(eSue) ? "" : "\nscore " + str.tostring(eSue, "#.0"))
    label.new(bar_index, diff >= 0 ? low : high, txtL, style = diff >= 0 ? label.style_label_up : label.style_label_down,
              color = eDir > 0 ? color.new(#30d158, 10) : eDir < 0 ? color.new(#ff453a, 10) : color.new(#8e8e93, 20), textcolor = color.white, size = size.small)

inWin = not na(eBar) and bar_index - eBar < driftLen
bgcolor(inWin and eDir > 0 ? color.new(#30d158, 90) : inWin and eDir < 0 ? color.new(#ff453a, 90) : na, title = "Drift window")

drift  = na(base) ? na : (close / base - 1) * 100
bDrift = na(bBase) or na(bClose) ? na : (bClose / bBase - 1) * 100
abn    = na(drift) or na(bDrift) ? na : drift - bDrift

alertcondition(isNew and eDir > 0, "Big beat", "{{ticker}}: big earnings beat, the drift window starts")
alertcondition(isNew and eDir < 0, "Big miss", "{{ticker}}: big earnings miss, the drift window starts")

var table tb = table.new(position.top_right, 2, 6, bgcolor = color.new(#1d1d1f, 0), border_width = 1, border_color = color.new(#2c2c2e, 0))
if showTbl and barstate.islast
    lc = color.new(#86868b, 0)
    vc = color.new(#f5f5f7, 0)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 0, "Last surprise", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 0, na(eSurp) ? "n/a" : (eSurp >= 0 ? "+" : "") + str.tostring(eSurp, "#.0") + "%", text_color = vc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 1, "Score (SUE-style)", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 1, na(eSue) ? "n/a" : str.tostring(eSue, "#.0"), text_color = vc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 2, "Days since report", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 2, na(eBar) ? "n/a" : str.tostring(bar_index - eBar), text_color = vc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 3, "Stock since report", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 3, na(drift) ? "n/a" : (drift >= 0 ? "+" : "") + str.tostring(drift, "#.0") + "%", text_color = vc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 4, "vs benchmark", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 4, na(abn) ? "n/a" : (abn >= 0 ? "+" : "") + str.tostring(abn, "#.0") + " pts", text_color = na(abn) ? vc : abn >= 0 ? color.new(#30d158, 0) : color.new(#ff453a, 0), text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 0, 5, "Window", text_color = lc, text_size = size.small)
    table.cell(tb, 1, 5, inWin ? (eDir > 0 ? "drift up" : eDir < 0 ? "drift down" : "small surprise") : "closed", text_color = vc, text_size = size.small)

Questions

What is SUE?

Standardized unexpected earnings: reported earnings minus what was expected, divided by how much the company's earnings usually miss the forecast.

How strong was the drift in the paper?

The long-short made money in 41 of 48 quarters from 1974 to 1985, and 25 to 30% of the drift landed at the next earnings reports.

Who found it first?

Ball and Brown, in 1968.

Does post-earnings drift still work?

Weakly in our test. The biggest surprises beat the biggest misses by about 1% over 60 days, positive in only 11 of 20 quarters, though our surprise date came from the later 10-Q filing.

Try it on paper first.

Pick the 20 biggest beats of the next earnings season, scored against each company's own history. Note the close the day after each report, and check them against SPY at 20, 40 and 60 trading days. Do it again next season before putting money on it.

  • Decide what counts as a big surprise before the season starts.
  • Note which ones held the gap and which faded it.
  • Write down whether you'd hold through the next report.
