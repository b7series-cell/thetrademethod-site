Let Winners Run

Most traders already cut their losers fine. They lose money by cutting their winners short, selling at a fixed target while the stock keeps going. Same entries, same stops: the only difference is whether a target or the move itself decides when you get out.

Same entries, different exits

1

Your losers aren't the problem

  • Put yourself next to a profitable trader on the same setup. You'd both buy at the same price, and if it goes against you, you'd both be stopped out at the same price.
  • The gap opens on the winners. You have a static profit target and you take it. They let the momentum, strength and direction of the stock decide where they get out.
  • So on every trade that works, they make more than you do, and nothing else needs to change.

Profitability is a two-part equation: the size of your average winner against the size of your average loser. If your losers are already in check, the only lever left is the winners.

2

Expected value, not win rate

  • Expected value per trade = win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss. That's what decides your result over your next 1,000 trades, not what this one trade does.
  • Letting winners run will lower your win rate. You'll hold through more trades that come back and stop you out. That's expected, and it's fine.
  • A 60% win rate making 1R per winner earns +0.2R a trade. Drop to 40% while tripling the average winner to 3R, and it's +0.6R a trade: three times the money.

Better exit rules are easier to learn than better pattern recognition. Stop worrying about how often you're right, and start caring how much you make when you are.

What changes when you stop selling winners

Two numbers move in opposite directions. Only one of them pays you.

Win rate goes down

You'll hold through pullbacks that sometimes turn into stops. More trades end red. Expect it.

Average winner goes up

The trades that work aren't capped at your target any more. One runner can pay for several small losses.

Expected value goes up

Win rate × a much bigger winner beats a higher win rate × a small one. That's the number to watch.

Rule 1 · One Below

The simplest of the three, and it works as both your stop and your profit exit from the moment you're in.

Out on the first close below the previous low

3

Exit on the first close below the previous candle's low

  • Every candle sets the line for the next one: its low. If the next candle closes below that low, you're out on that close.
  • It has to close there. A candle that trades below the previous low and closes back above it is just a wick, and you stay in.
  • It's your stop too. If the trade goes against you right away, the first close below the previous low takes you out with a small loss.
  • It's simple on purpose. A rule you can spot and act on in real time beats a clever one you can't execute live.

The close-only condition is what keeps you in strong moves: bottoming wicks shake out traders who exit on a touch, but they don't trigger this rule.

Rule 2 · ATR trail

An exit that gives a volatile stock more room and a quiet one less, measured in the stock's own dollars.

Highest high of the last 10 candles, minus ATR

4

Trail = highest high of the previous 10 candles − ATR(14)

  • ATR, average true range, measures how far each candle has typically moved over the last 14, in dollars. It's a live read on how volatile the stock is right now.
  • A volatile stock needs room to breathe, because a sharp dip can be noise before the next leg. A calm one doesn't, so the stop can sit closer.
  • Take the highest high of the previous 10 candles and subtract the ATR. That's your line. Every new high lifts it.
  • Exit on a candle that closes below the line. Candles that trade below it and close back above don't count.

Anchoring to the recent high keeps the exit following the move up, and sizing the gap with ATR keeps it from being too tight on a wild stock or too loose on a quiet one.

Rule 3 · Key levels

The discretionary version, and the most common way traders let winners run: you choose the levels, the closes decide the exit.

Raise the exit to each new pause

5

Exit on a close below the latest pause

  • After the entry, never sell into strength. Let the stock run.
  • When it pauses, pulls back or chops sideways, mark the bottom of that pause as your level.
  • When it breaks out again and forms a new pause, move your level up to the new one.
  • Exit on a candle that closes below the current level.

This one isn't a coded rule. You decide where the levels are as the chart develops, which suits discretionary traders and frustrates systematic ones.

Systematic or discretionary?

Two ways to run any of these exits. Neither is wrong, as long as you never sell a green candle out of nerves.

Systematic

Exact, predefined reasons to enter and exit: One Below and the ATR trail. Same input, same exit, every time.

Discretionary

Live chart reading on momentum, strength and direction. Key levels: you draw them, so two traders can exit in different places.

Either way

The exit waits for the move to weaken. It never fires because you're up a round number.

Trade like a surfer

The metaphor that ties all three rules together.

6

Catch the wave early, ride it until it fades

  • A surfer doesn't paddle out to open water and hope a wave shows up. They wait for one to start, then get on it right away. Don't buy stocks drifting sideways and hope; wait for the move to begin and get in on its first candle.
  • Nobody calls it a good ride if the surfer jumps off halfway. Don't sell at +4%, +7% or +10% just because those are nice numbers.
  • Ride until the wave starts to dissipate, and let a systematic rule define that moment, as early as it can honestly be seen.

There's always some giveback at the end of a ride. It's still the best way to grow the average winner, and the average winner drives expected value.

The ride, in three moments

Every exit rule on this page fits the same shape.

Paddle in as it starts

Enter on the first candle of the move, not before it and not three candles late.

Stay on through the curl

No green candle gets sold. Wicks and small dips are part of the wave.

Step off as it dissipates

A close below the previous low, the ATR line or your key level. Then, and only then, you're out.

Four habits that cap your winners

Each one feels like discipline. Each one quietly lowers your average winner.

1  "I'm up 5%, take it"

A fixed target sells the trade at exactly the moment it's proving itself, and the runner goes on without you.

Kills: the trades that would have paid for ten losers.

2  Selling a green candle

If the candle in front of you is closing green, nothing has weakened yet. Every rule here waits for weakness first.

Kills: momentum you already paid the risk to own.

3  Exiting on a touch

Getting out the instant price pokes through a level. All three rules act on the close, so wicks don't shake you out.

Kills: strong trends with deep intrabar wicks.

4  Chasing a higher win rate

Judging yourself by how often you're right. A higher hit rate with tiny winners can earn less than a lower one with big winners.

Kills: your expected value over the next 1,000 trades.

You can't control which trades work. You can control whether the ones that do are allowed to become big winners. That's the part of expected value you own.
