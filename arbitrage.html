What Arbitrage Is

Arbitrage is buying something where it's cheap and selling the exact same thing where it's expensive, at the same time. No waiting for the price to move: the gap already exists. And almost all of the easy money already goes to the fastest machines.

What is arbitrage?

Buying an asset where it's cheap and selling the same asset where it's more expensive, at the same time, to profit from the price gap. If something trades at $100 on one exchange and $105 on another, buying one and selling the other earns $5 a unit before fees.

How the gap closes

1

Spatial arbitrage

  • The simplest kind: the same asset in different places or on different exchanges.
  • An arbitrage trader, most likely a computer, buys on exchange A for $100 and sells on exchange B for $105.
  • That's why people call arbitrage "risk-free profit". Hold that thought.

If markets are so efficient, why would the same thing ever trade at two prices? Small imperfections open brief windows, sometimes milliseconds, sometimes minutes.

Why gaps appear

Different exchanges

Crypto exchanges and stock exchanges in different countries don't all move at exactly the same speed.

Different regions

An asset can be slightly cheaper in one country because of local supply, taxes or capital controls.

Information lag

News hits one exchange a fraction of a second before another, and big players see order book updates before the public feed does.

Three kinds you'll hear about

There are several types of arbitrage. These are the big ones.

Triangular

Dollars to euros, euros to yen, yen back to dollars. If the three rates don't quite line up, you end with more than you started.

Happens in forex, in tiny amounts.
takeovers

Merger (risk) arbitrage

After a takeover offer, the target's stock usually trades a bit below the offer price. Buy it and bet the deal closes.

Called risk arbitrage because the deal can fail and the stock can drop.

Around the triangle

2

Merger arbitrage, with made-up numbers

  • A company offers to buy another for $50 a share. The target trades at $48.
  • Buy at $48. If the deal closes, it's worth $50: +$2 a share.
  • If the deal falls apart, the stock can drop back toward where it was before the offer.

The small, likely gain against a bigger, less likely loss is the whole trade. That's why it's called risk arbitrage.

In the real world, arbitrage is low risk, but definitely not zero risk. — Martik Finance

Calling it truly risk-free is marketing

Arbitrage is usually much lower risk than betting on direction. But four things can and do go wrong.

Execution

By the time your buy fills on the cheap exchange, the price on the expensive one has already dropped.

Half a trade isn't arbitrage.

Fees

A 0.2% fee on each side can eat most of a 1 to 2% gap.

Small gap, smaller profit.

Liquidity

Only $2,000 is available at the good price, and you wanted to do $50,000.

The gap is real; the size isn't.

Transfer delays

Especially in crypto, moving coins between exchanges can take minutes.

Plenty of time for the gap to vanish.

Who actually gets it

Today the vast majority of arbitrage profit goes to high-frequency trading firms, big hedge funds, bank trading desks and well-funded crypto market makers. By the time a retail trader spots a gap between two screens, it's usually gone.

A gap opens

  • No bet on direction: the profit is the gap.

Questions

Is arbitrage really risk-free?

In theory, yes. In practice it's low risk, not zero risk: the price can move before your order fills, fees can eat the gap, there may not be enough size at the good price, and transfers between exchanges take time.

Why do price gaps happen?

Different exchanges don't all move at the same speed, regions differ in supply, taxes or capital controls, and news reaches one market a fraction of a second before another.

What is triangular arbitrage?

A currency trade in a loop, such as dollars to euros, euros to yen and yen back to dollars. If the three exchange rates don't quite line up, you end with more than you started, before fees.

What is merger arbitrage?

After a takeover offer, the target's stock usually trades slightly below the offer price. Traders buy it, betting the deal closes. It's also called risk arbitrage because the deal can fail and the stock can drop.

Can retail traders profit from arbitrage?

Rarely. High-frequency firms with servers next to the exchange, direct data feeds, tiny fees and big capital usually close a gap within milliseconds, before a person could spot it.

Low risk, not no risk, and not for us.

Arbitrage is worth understanding because it's why prices on different screens agree. It isn't a strategy for a retail trader with a broker app: the gaps are gone before you see them, and the ones that last usually have a reason.
