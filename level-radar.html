What is Level Radar?

Level Radar is a TradingView indicator that shows, for ten levels day traders watch, the chance in percent that price touches each one before the session ends. The levels are the previous day's high, low and close, the overnight high and low, today's open, the opening range high and low, and today's high and low so far. The odds update on every bar.

The levels everyone watches, with a number on each

These areas often act like price magnets, and you see them revisited again and again through the trading day. What a plain line can't tell you is how likely that still is right now, with this much of the session left. For every level, Level Radar shows that chance in percent, and it updates with every bar.

The levels at the open

1

The ten levels

  • PDH, PDL, PDC: the previous session's high, low and close.
  • ONH, ONL: the high and low of the bars between the previous session and this one. Empty on full-day sessions and on charts without extended hours.
  • OPEN: today's open.
  • ORH, ORL: the high and low of the opening range, the first 30 minutes by default. They appear half an hour in, once the range is over.
  • HOD, LOD: today's high and low so far. Their odds are the chance of a new high or low before the session ends.

Many traders watch the opening range because the first minutes of a session often set the tone for the rest of the day. Use 15 minutes or an hour instead if that's your range.

Reading a label

Each level is a glowing line across today's session with a label on the right. The label tells you three things: the name, the price, and the chance of a touch before the session ends.

greenAbove price

Brighter and thicker means higher odds. The unlikely ones fade into the background.

redBelow price

Same rule. The levels that matter right now literally stand out.

✓ tick markAlready touched today

The percentage is then the chance price comes back to it once more.

ORL · ONLOne shared label

Two levels almost on top of each other share a label, so the chart doesn't clutter.

How the odds are worked out

Level Radar compares how far away a level is with how far price usually still travels in the time that's left today. Then it checks that against the past.

First, the distance

The odds, step by step

Five steps on every bar, for every level. Click through them.

1

Mark the levels

  • Yesterday's high, low and close, the overnight high and low, today's open, and the opening range once it's over.
  • Today's high and low so far ride along too, for the odds of a new extreme.

The levels most traders are already watching. Nothing new to learn.

2

Measure the distance

  • For every level, how far price would have to move from the last close to get there.
  • A level price is sitting on shows 100%.

Distance alone isn't enough. Twenty points is easy at the open and a lot at 3:30.

3

Size the room left today

  • Start from the average range of the last ten sessions.
  • Shrink it as the session runs out: less time means less room to move.
  • If today is moving more or less than usual, adjust for that too.

In the script: the typical range times the square root of the share of the session left, times today's range against the usual range by this time, capped between half and double.

4

Check the past

  • On many earlier days, at this time of day, how often did price still travel that far before the close?
  • Seven out of ten days makes 70%.

Those days come from fifteen years of index and gold futures built into the script, and from the last twenty sessions of your own chart.

5

Read the odds

  • A percent on every level, updated on every bar, and a panel that ranks them.
  • A nearby level in the morning shows a high number. A distant level late in the afternoon shows a low one.

One glance tells you where price is probably heading, and where it probably isn't.

Where the past days come from

Two sources, so the odds fit the market you're actually watching.

  • Built in: fifteen years of S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow futures (9:30 to 16:00 New York) and gold futures (8:20 to 13:30), 5-minute bars from 2011 to 2026. In the script that's one table: 16 time-of-day buckets by 61 distance steps.
  • Your chart: the last 20 completed sessions, split the same way, up and down kept apart. The script weighs them by sessions ÷ (sessions + 80), so 20 sessions count for 20% and the long-run table for the rest.
  • Crypto, forex and other full-day charts: no futures table. A statistical formula instead (the random-walk law), with the room left taken from a time-of-day profile of the last 60 days, blended with your chart's own sessions.

Are the levels really magnets?

The script keeps score on your chart. For each named level it scores one forecast per session (at the open, or when the opening range is set): the average forecast against the share that was actually touched.

The script's own note on the scoreboard: in a 15-year study on index futures and gold, named levels were touched as often as any other price at the same distance.

So the odds come from distance and time, not from the name on the line. That's useful in itself: it tells you which of the levels you already watch are realistic today.

The formula

Room left = average range × √(share of session left) × pace, with pace capped between 0.5× and 2×. Pace = today's range ÷ (average range × √share of session gone).

Chance of a touch = 2 × (1 − Φ(1.596 × distance ÷ room left)), where Φ is the normal curve and 1.596 is how a random walk's expected range compares to its standard deviation.

Half the usual range away at the open: about 42%. A quarter: about 69%, close to the 70% in the video's example.

Time is part of the number

All of the odds run until the end of the session. A level that looked easy to reach in the morning can become a long shot by the afternoon, even if price never moves.

The same distance, worth less every hour

By default it picks the session for you. If you trade a different window, switch to Full day, or choose Custom and type in your own start, end and time zone.

Stocks

Everything else

Crypto, forex and the rest: the full trading day.

Three ways to use it

Level Radar is not a signal generator. It doesn't tell you when to buy or sell. It gives you context, and it works best combined with the analysis you already do.

Your setup says long

1 · targets

Realistic profit targets

Your strategy gives a long. If yesterday's high sits above you at 70%, that's a realistic target. At 15%, aim for something closer.

2 · filter

Betting against the odds

If your setup only works when price reaches a level the radar puts at 10 or 20%, the trade can still work. It's a reason to think twice, or to size down.

3 · timing

Watch the numbers drop

The odds shrink as the session runs out. Watching them fall keeps you from holding on to a target the market no longer has time to reach.

not this

Hold or break

It doesn't tell you what happens when price gets there. A level can hold or break. That part is market structure, trend, volume, order flow, your method.

The indicator

Level Radar [GBB] by The Good, The Bad And The Bitcoin, free and open source on TradingView (Pine Script v6). It draws the levels and their odds, the panel and the scoreboard, and alerts on touches. It doesn't place trades. A NinjaTrader version is linked from the video.

  • Level Radar [GBB]

    alerts

    PDH, PDL, PDC, ONH, ONL, OPEN, ORH, ORL, HOD and LOD with the chance of a touch before the session ends, a ranking panel with a session clock, and a scoreboard that checks its own forecasts on your chart.

    Works on intraday charts from 1 to 60 minutes. It needs three completed sessions before the odds start (“Warming up”), and gets better as up to 20 of your chart's sessions fill its table. A touch counts when a bar's range, including the gap from the previous close, covers the level. Session length is the most common of the last five sessions.

// Most common value; ties go to the most recent one.
modeRecent(array<float> a) =>
    float best = na
    int bestN = 0
    for i = a.size() - 1 to 0
        float v = a.get(i)
        int n = 0
        for j = 0 to a.size() - 1
            if a.get(j) == v
                n += 1
        if n > bestN
            bestN := n
            best := v
    best

// tail[r * (NB + 2) + k] = sum of row r's bins k .. NB; one extra 0 closes each row.
tails(array<float> hist, int nrow) =>
    array<float> t = array.new<float>(nrow * (NB + 2), 0.0)
    for r = 0 to nrow - 1
        float acc = 0.0
        for k = NB to 0
            acc += hist.get(r * (NB + 1) + k)
            t.set(r * (NB + 2) + k, acc)
    t

// Share of samples >= x in row r, linear inside a bin; na when the row is empty.
surv(array<float> hist, array<float> tail, int r, float x) =>
    float n = tail.get(r * (NB + 2))
    float res = na
    if n > 0
        float q = math.min(x / BW, NB + 1.0)
        int k = math.min(int(math.floor(q)), NB)
        float frac = k < NB ? q - k : 0.0
        res := (tail.get(r * (NB + 2) + k + 1) + (1.0 - frac) * hist.get(r * (NB + 1) + k)) / n
    res

// Random-walk law 2 * (1 - Phi(C_RW * x)), Abramowitz-Stegun 7.1.26.

    float u = C_RW * x / math.sqrt(2.0)

    float poly = k * (0.254829592 + k * (-0.284496736 + k * (1.421413741 + k * (-1.453152027 + k * 1.061405429))))
    poly * math.exp(-u * u)

// Chance that the level is touched in the rest of the window. na: no level or still warming up.
oddsOf(float lv, float scale, int bucket) =>
    float res = na
    if not na(lv) and not na(scale)
        if lv == close
            res := 1.0
        else if not (scale > 0)
            res := 0.0
        else
            float x = math.abs(lv - close) / scale
            int d = lv < close ? 1 : 0
            float n = nValid
            float pr = DAY ? s0(x) : surv(PH, PT, bucket, x)
            float sPool = n > 0 ? surv(pool, poolTail, 0, x) : na
            float sBd = n > 0 ? surv(tab, tabTail, bucket * 2 + d, x) : na
            float w2 = DAY ? n / (n + M2) : 0.0
            float pooled = na(sPool) ? pr : w2 * sPool + (1.0 - w2) * pr
            float w1 = DAY or na(sBd) ? 0.0 : n / (n + M1)
            res := w1 * nz(sBd) + (1.0 - w1) * pooled
    res

Questions

How are the odds worked out?

It compares each level's distance with how far price usually still travels in the time left today, using the last ten sessions' range, shrunk as the session runs out, then checks how often price went that far on past days at the same time of day.

What do the colors mean?

Green levels are above price and red below. Brighter and thicker means higher odds. A tick mark means the level was already touched today.

Where does the history come from?

Fifteen years of index and gold futures built into the script, plus the last twenty sessions of your own chart.

Are Level Radar's odds accurate?

On SPY and QQQ, in our check, yes: levels marked 60 to 70% were touched about 62% of the time, usually within 1 to 3 points. On single stocks the middle-range odds ran 8 to 11 points too high.

Before your next target, ask what the odds are.

Not whether the level is important. Whether price still has the room and the time to get there today. A nearby level in the morning is a realistic target. A distant one late in the afternoon is a hope. Then let your own method decide what to do when price arrives.

  • The odds run to the end of the session. Set the session right.
  • Room left = the usual range, shrunk by the time left, adjusted for today's pace.
  • Seven of ten earlier days got there = 70%.
  • Targets: aim where the odds are realistic.
  • Filter: a 10 to 20% level means think twice, or size down.
  • Timing: watch the odds fall as the day runs out.
  • Not a signal: hold or break is your call.
