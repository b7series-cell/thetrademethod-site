Pyramiding Into Winners

How do you add to a winner and end up risking less than when you started? The first entry is a full risk unit. Every add is smaller than the one before it, and every add moves the stop in your favor. The size grows. The risk falls.

Adding to a loser is not a strategy

You buy a stock, it goes against you, and instead of taking the loss you buy more because now it's cheaper. That feels like discipline. It isn't. It's adding to a loser and calling it a strategy, and over time it takes a large bite out of your capital. Pyramiding is the opposite, and it's the thing almost nobody does well.

1

The trap on the winning side

  • You're in a trade that's working. The stock is doing exactly what you wanted, how you wanted, when you wanted.
  • The second entry looks obvious, and the voice in your head says, "I'm already up. I can afford it." That voice is the trap.
  • Most traders undersize the first trade and oversize the second one, and every add after it. They try to get paid more because the trade is working.

That's how most people get pyramiding wrong: too little risk on the first entry, too much on the add.

Pyramiding is not martingale

The distinction that makes everything else work.

martingaleBigger bets against you

The trade goes wrong and you add more, typically doubling the bet, then doubling again until it works. A dangerous strategy. On the SMB desk, someone doing it is kindly invited to leave.

pyramidingSmaller bets with you

You add only after favorable movement, and every add is smaller than the one before it from a risk perspective. You never increase past your original risk.

The math, in risk units

Talk about everything in R to keep it simple. One R is the most you're willing to lose on the trade. The first entry gets 1R. Every add gets a fraction of that, and the stop moves up behind it.

1R, then 0.5R, then 0.25R

2

Smaller every time, stop up every time

  • First entry on a breakout of a range: 1R, with the stop below the low of the range.
  • Price goes up, pulls back and consolidates. If it gives you another trade, the add is a fraction of the first size. Never more than 1R. Maybe 0.5R.
  • As you add, move the stop up to the prior support the add came from. Every add after that, the stop moves in your favor and the size goes down.

That's the whole trick. By the third entry you can be holding your biggest position of the trade and risking less than on the very first entry.

Four outcomes, and the math picks

If the trade fails, you stop out small, and if you stop out small you're likely in profit. If it runs, your biggest size is on for the real move. Done right, the occasional winner can run to 20 or 25R, not because you predicted a home run, but because the math lets you stay in with size while the risk keeps shrinking.

The stop has moved up behind every add, so a failure costs little or nothing.

The model: an add on a fashionably late trade

A drawing of the structure first, with no ticker and no prices, because the model is where the math is cleanest. The first entry follows the fashionably late rules. The add comes near target, and it has to earn its place.

Up 3R: add half, not three

3

Open profit is not a cushion

  • Near target, most traders see the open profit and treat it like a cushion: "This is working better than I thought, I can add big."
  • Instead of just taking it off into target, you might take an offside scalp in the direction of the trade: a second entry at a really good location.
  • Then move the entire stop up to where the initial target was.

Up 3R and risking 0.5R on the add, the worst case is still up 2.5R. Risk another 3R on the add and a failure wipes out every bit of profit. That's what puts more traders on tilt than anything else, and it's mentally fatiguing.

The question the add has to answer

Is the structure stronger at the add than it was at the entry? If yes, it's a new, independently valid setup at a better location, and you can put more risk on. But it's a smaller second entry: half a risk unit, not a whole one.

Think in R, start with halves

Halves are an honest way to start. A dollar stop is fine, but when you think about every trade in risk units you can pyramid into trades consistently.

A recent example from the desk. An opening range break long, a structure that builds, and one add at half a risk unit. Watch what happens to the risk when the whole stop moves up.

The puppy dog add

4

Where the add came from

  • Right after the entry? Not really. Some people take a 9 EMA continuation there, but he likes the structure to be built.
  • A small "puppy dog" consolidation above the highs of the recent pullback, then a break on increased volume: he loves that spot for an add.
  • Stop below the low of the consolidation. Clean, 4 points of risk.

Not increasing size randomly, not adding without a plan, half-sizing all the way up: that's how you keep adding while protecting against giving back too much open P&L, and protect your mindset, which matters most.

The VWAP add

5

Keep stepping down

  • The add doesn't have to risk to the highs. A clear, distinct turn against VWAP is a more conservative place to stand.
  • CBRS only gave one chance to add, but you could keep going: 0.25R ($250), then less.
  • If you keep pyramiding, 0.1R is a good lowest tier.

Every entry goes down in risk units, so you're protected all the way. You get the experience of adding to a winner without the "I was up a ton and gave it all back" pitfalls.

An add on volume alone isn't pyramiding

One contrast, because it will save you money. Both examples above passed the same test: the add earned its place on the structure, not on the open P&L. A volume spike with no technical location of its own doesn't.

Adding risk on hope

Before every add, ask out loud

Write it down, because the moment you need it is the moment your gut will argue with it.

question one

Is the idea still valid?

And is it playing out better than expected (BTE)? Is the price action doing exactly what you hoped for?

question two

Would I take it on its own?

If you weren't already in the original trade, would you take this one? And what is your total risk in the position now?

No number or risk unit for the total? Don't add. Wait.
sizing

Sized for its own structure

The add has its own level, its own stop and its own size, with the whole position's risk checked before you click.

mindset

Don't fall in love with the add

The first entry was just a trade. The second is a new trade that happens to sit next to it. Same standard, same skepticism.

Most adds won't earn their place. The ones that do are the ones that pay.

The pyramid, step by step

The whole technique in five steps. Click through them.

1

First entry: 1R

  • Put the appropriate risk on the first trade: a full risk unit, to the stop the setup calls for.
  • Don't enter thinking "I hope I can pyramid this."

Undersizing the first entry is half of how traders get pyramiding wrong.

2

Let the tape hand you the add

  • Wait for structure: a pullback, a consolidation, a level the add can stand on.
  • You don't hunt for adds. The tape hands you one, or it doesn't.

That's the difference between a rule and a habit you force.

3

Ask the two questions

  • Is the trade idea still valid, and is it playing out better than expected?
  • Is this a new trade I'd take on its own? And what's my total risk in the position?

If you can't answer the total risk with a number or a risk unit, you don't add.

4

Add smaller each time

  • Never more than the original risk. Start with halves: 0.5R, then 0.25R.
  • If you keep pyramiding, 0.1R is the lowest tier.

The add is sized for its own structure, never for the open profit.

5

Move the stop, check the total

  • Every add moves the stop for the whole position in your favor.
  • Check the whole position's risk before you click. It should be going down.

The size grows naturally. The risk falls the entire time.

Pull up your last winning trade.

Was the add you took, or the add you wanted to take, smaller in risk units than the first entry? Did your total risk go down when you took it? If not, you weren't pyramiding. You were averaging in the other direction, which is gambling. You can fix it on the very next one.

  • First entry: a full 1R.
  • Add only after favorable movement, never against you.
  • The add has to be a new valid setup at a better location.
  • Each add smaller: 0.5R, 0.25R, down to 0.1R.
  • Move the whole stop in your favor with every add.
  • No level, no stop of its own? It's hope, not an add.
  • Know your total risk before you click.
