What is auction market theory?

Auction market theory describes the market as an auction that moves through three phases: balance, where price rotates between two walls; transition, where big orders accept value outside the range; and imbalance, a trend toward the next area of value where a new balance forms. On this page, from Fabio Valentini's video, the first step is reading which regime the market is in before picking a trade.

Step one: which regime is the market in?

The first question to ask of every market, even a trending one like the Nasdaq or Bitcoin: what's actually dominating right now? Auction market theory answers it with three phases, and it tells you what to expect from the coming week and from each single-day auction.

1

A trending asset can still be in balance

  • Through the summer the Nasdaq revolved around a three-month rotation on the composite profile.
  • The range was extremely choppy: the market kept running to one extreme, getting rejected, and running to the other.
  • Volatility took the biggest hit. Price moved overnight, after the day's zero-days-to-expiry options had expired, and sat nearly still during the day.

Knowing that regime in advance tells you what kind of week, and what kind of session, you're walking into.

2

The market is an elastic band

  • Auction market theory is phases of extension and phases of snapback.
  • It starts from balance: every market has to build order and complete its accumulation and distribution.
  • The market is a pool of operators with different objectives and needs. Rotating around one area isn't just compression, it's building the fuel for a directional move.

Balance, transition, imbalance

3

What ends a balance

  • Watch for the moment the market goes from phase A to phase B: balance, then transition, then imbalance.
  • This year there was almost always a fundamental catalyst behind it: a presidential post, a war threat between Iran and the US, a change from the Fed, strong earnings in tech.

Long term, the fundamentals are the reason a market leaves balance. The auction tells you when it's actually happening.

Trading inside balance: wall to wall

Auction market theory also tells you which model fits the market. A trend follower in a balanced market can't push reward-to-risk to big numbers, because more often than not the market bounces off the walls above and below.

Wall to wall, not 1:5

1

Short-term trend following, inside the range

  • Price taps the upper wall. Trapped buyers from big orders, or strong selling aggression as it comes back inside, give you the trade.
  • The rejection prints an initiative auction candle: a strong aggression candle. That's a short-term trend-following trade to the other side of the range.
  • Know where the path of least resistance stops: at the other wall.
2

Then expect the opposite

  • At the other boundary, expect accumulation: big orders absorbing, or strong absorption on the delta profile. Read order flow with whatever tool you prefer.
  • When price re-enters balance off that wall, the next trade goes back to the other side.

"This is only 1:1.5 or 1:2." Right. Every market phase comes with its own potential direction, and in balance it isn't clear.

Mean reversion still pays

Some summer days you could close at +5, +6 or +7R just mean-reverting inside the area. The one thing not to do: once the market accepts value below the area, stop trying to buy. It usually accelerates in that direction.

The transition candle and the asymmetrical opportunity

When a candle finally accepts value outside the area, after six hours in a day or a few days in a week, big orders are switching the regime from balance to imbalance. That's where higher reward-to-risk becomes possible, depending on what's above.

The asymmetrical daily opportunity

1

What makes it asymmetrical

  • Take the pre-market profile, the three hours before the New York open. In the first hours sellers defend the top and buyers defend the bottom.
  • Above it there's an empty, low volume node, and the next value, the next balance, is far away.
  • If value moves up, there's all that room before the next place the market wants to trade.

He takes this once the market shows its hand, usually with the initial volume breakout: the first time the buyers show their intention.

2

Mark the delta peak and the value area low

  • Take a profile of just the move from point A to point B. Zoom in: the levels adapt to how granular you go.
  • Mark the delta peak, where buyers were most aggressive, and the value area low, the most important level, in green.
  • Two levels sit close above and below: that's your margin of error. Mark it on both sides.

Long term, the most relevant switches of the auction all come from a delta peak. You're asking the market: show me where buyers were most aggressive.

Two ways to place the stop

tightUnder the aggression

Just under the area where buyers got aggressive, past the delta peak's margin of error. Bigger reward-to-risk, stopped more often.

relaxedUnder the bulk of delta

Use the profile from A to B to see where most of the buyers' aggression was built, and protect below all of it. In the swing example this was about a 1:3 to the local top.

Enter on the test of the big trade

Fabio's position: enter when price tests the big trade, protect below the bulk of the buyers' delta, and go for the expansion to the top side. Opportunities like this don't come every day. Sometimes the market just goes around the consolidation and never offers one.

Manage it, then build with a third of the locked profit

The long goes well, then the market starts to rotate and buyers and sellers build a new area of battle. How much momentum is left? You either take the position off as soon as the sellers kick in, or you manage it, and if the next balance is far away, build.

Position building, worst case still green

1

Don't close the whole thing just because you touched a level

  • If buyers are extremely aggressive and you get acceptance at the top of the range, with big trades buying, you can stay with it.
  • Bring all the floating profit to the last invalidation point: move the whole stop there.
  • Now that profit is locked in, and you can use part of it to build another position.
2

Divide it in three

  • Say the locked profit is $3,000. Take one third, $1,000, and risk it on an additional trend-following position.
  • Worst case the market comes back: you lose 1R on the add, but you still keep the rest of the locked profit.
  • Best case you get a directional market, and an asymmetrical session of 6 or 8R.

One or two sessions like that a week is enough to close an excellent week.

Only when the next balance is far

Building makes sense when, after this balance, the next one is really far away. That's what keeps it an asymmetrical opportunity. If the next value sits right above you, take the position off when the sellers show up.

Reading each session's auction

In practice, start from the long-term profile, then go day by day: who dominated the auction, and what does that mean for the next regular trading hours? In Fabio's Nasdaq example a level at 30,333 acted as the wall, and once value was accepted above it the market expanded hard.

Who won yesterday's auction?

1

Accepted lower: expect a sell auction

  • The previous value got breached on the downside, the POC is lower and the aggression already broke the value area low.
  • Expect the regular session to be an aggressive sell auction, expanding at least to the local low.
2

A failed auction is a tail

  • At a level you marked before, buyers get aggressive. First rejection. A second aggression makes a lower POC and tries to go into imbalance, but the profile can't accept below the absorption.
  • It's like an inside candle: I tried to go lower and I failed. Then value is accepted above the previous session's bulk of volume.

Then look for longs next session: fade the value area low in the direction of the pressure.

Merge sessions when single ones confuse you

Taken one by one, five sessions can be confusing. Merge them into one range and you're adding up the volume of several sessions to see what to expect. Single profiles and the composite together show which area is still being respected, like the value area low that was used for the buy in the video.

Swing or single auction

  • Swing: a relaxed position protected below the delta peak, to the local top. About a 1:3.
  • Single auction: wait for the candle that finally accepts higher, then scalp in the direction of the higher time frame.

Candle framing, intraday

The same logic works one step down, on the intraday profile. Fabio's candle framing template puts a profile on each candle of a range chart, so you can see who won each little auction.

The change of flow

1

What is a change of flow?

  • A candle's POC lower than the one before: sellers still in control.
  • Buyers try to take control back and fail: sellers still in control.
  • Then the POC migrates higher and value is accepted above. Now there's no doubt.

Fabio usually goes for a 1:2 here, taking the expansion area at the top because they accepted value.

Stack it on what you already do

All of this sits on top of your other studies. Fabio pairs it with a study he calls the Nasdaq effort, which takes the buyers' point of control when they're pushing in the same direction, on a 40 range chart. If it marks buyer aggression inside the area you were already looking at, that's an accumulation area to trade in the direction of the main bias. You can also time the entry with your own order flow.

If you started from zero

The order Fabio would learn it in again: auction market theory and order flow first, then option flow on top. Click through the five steps.

1

Identify the regime

  • Before anything else: is this market in balance, in transition, or in imbalance?
  • Check the composite profile for the week and the daily profile for the session.

It's the single most important question, and it picks the model for you.

2

In balance, trade wall to wall

  • Initiative auction candles off one wall, absorption at the other.
  • Keep targets at the other wall: 1:1.5 to 1:2. A 1:5 in a rotation is a string of stops.

Protecting capital comes first.

3

Wait for the transition candle

  • Big orders accepting value outside the area: the switch from balance to imbalance.
  • Check what's above: a low volume node and a far-away next value make the opportunity asymmetrical.

Once value is accepted below an area, stop trying to buy it.

4

Mark the delta peak and the VAL

  • Profile the move from A to B. Mark the delta peak and the value area low, plus a margin of error on each side.
  • Enter on the test of the big trade, stop below the bulk of the buyers' delta.

The biggest switches of the auction come from these delta peaks.

5

Manage, then build

  • New area of battle: exit when sellers kick in, or move the whole stop to the last invalidation.
  • Risk one third of the locked profit on an add. Worst case you still close green.

One or two directional days a week make the week.

Questions

Can a trending market be in balance?

Yes. Through one summer the Nasdaq rotated inside a three-month range on the composite profile, running to one extreme, getting rejected and running to the other.

What ends a balance?

A transition candle, also called an initiative auction, where big orders accept value outside the area. Long term, a fundamental catalyst is usually the reason a market leaves balance.

How do you trade inside balance?

Wall to wall. Trade the rejection at one wall toward the other, then expect the opposite at the other boundary. The realistic target is about 1:1.5 to 1:2, not 1:5.

How much should you risk on an add?

About a third of the profit you've already locked in.

Learn the auction first. Strategies come and go.

An edge can disappear and a strategy can stop working. Understanding how the market auctions, and which phase it's in, stays with you. Start there, add order flow, and build everything else on top.

  • Name the regime before the trade: balance, transition or imbalance.
  • In balance, target the other wall: 1:1.5 to 1:2.
  • Value accepted outside the area? That's the transition candle.
  • Far next value plus a thin path: an asymmetrical opportunity.
  • Delta peak and value area low, with a margin of error. Stop beyond them.
  • Lock the profit at the last invalidation, then risk one third to build.
  • Value accepted below the area? Stop trying to buy.
