How Futures Work

A futures contract is a deal to buy or sell something at a set price on a set date. Both sides are on the hook, a small deposit controls a big position, and the account is settled in cash every single day. That's the opportunity and the danger in one sentence.

How do futures work?

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell a set amount of something at a set price on a set date. Unlike an option, both sides are obligated. You put up a small deposit called margin, often around 5% of the contract's value, and your account is marked to market every day, so gains and losses are paid in cash daily.

What a futures contract is

An agreement between two people to buy or sell something at a set price on a set date in the future. The video starts with an airline that has already sold next year's tickets and has no idea what jet fuel will cost. A futures contract lets it lock tomorrow's price today.

The wheat deal

1

A deal both sides must keep

  • It's March. The farmer agrees to sell 5,000 bushels of wheat in September at $6 a bushel.
  • The bread company agrees to buy at that price, no matter what wheat does in between.
  • Unless you close it out before it expires, you can't walk away.

That's the big difference from an option. An option gives you the right to buy or sell, and you can walk away losing only the premium. A futures contract is an obligation for both sides.

Futures vs options

futuresAn obligation

Both sides must go through with it, or close it before it expires. Gains and losses have no built-in cap.

optionsA right

The buyer can walk away. The most the buyer can lose is the premium paid.

Who's on each side

Hedgers

Businesses locking in a price to kill uncertainty: the farmer, the airline buying jet fuel, a jewelry company that needs to know what gold will cost. They aren't trying to get rich off the swings.

Speculators

Traders who never want a bushel of wheat or a tanker of crude. They take the other side of the hedger's risk to profit from the price move itself. Without them the hedger has no one to trade with.

What you can trade with futures

Futures started with crops and raw materials. Today financial futures make up a huge share of the volume.

commodity

Physical goods

Crude oil, gold, wheat, natural gas.

Some settle by physical delivery if you hold to expiration.
stock index

The whole market

Contracts on the S&P 500 or the Nasdaq.

Speculate on, or hedge against, the entire market moving up or down.
rates & currency

Bonds and FX

Interest rate futures on US Treasuries, and currency futures on exchange rates.

Mostly used by institutions to hedge.
crypto

The newest kind

Futures on Bitcoin, Ethereum and other coins.

Trade the price without holding coins in a wallet.

Long and short are equally normal

In stocks most people only think one way: buy low, sell high. In futures you're just choosing a side of the agreement, so selling first is as easy as buying.

2

Pick a side of the deal

  • Long: you buy the contract, locking in today's price to buy later. You win if the price rises.
  • Short: you sell the contract first, without owning anything, locking in today's price to sell later. You win if the price falls.
  • To get out, take the opposite trade: a long sells the same contract, a short buys it back.

Your profit or loss is the difference between entry and exit, times the contract size. That's why the contract size is the first thing to look up.

If you hold to expiration

physical deliveryThe goods show up

You deliver, or take delivery of, the real thing. One crude contract is 1,000 barrels.

cash settledThe difference is paid

No goods change hands. The price difference is paid out in cash.

That's why most traders close well before expiration. Very few people want 100 ounces of gold or 1,000 barrels of oil at their door.

Contract size and tick value

Every contract is a fixed, standard amount set by the exchange. You can't customize it. That amount decides how many dollars you make or lose for every move in price.

3

Look these up before any new contract

  • Crude oil (CME): 1,000 barrels. A $1 move is $1,000. The tick is 1 cent, worth $10.
  • Gold: 100 troy ounces. A $1 move is $100.
  • E-mini S&P 500: the index times $50. The tick is a quarter point, worth $12.50.

The tick is the smallest move a contract is allowed to make. Tick value is the contract size times the tick: 1,000 barrels × $0.01 = $10.

What a move is worth

Pick a contract, then set the entry, exit and number of contracts.

Margin is a deposit, not a loan

In stocks, margin means borrowing from your broker and paying interest. In futures, margin is a security deposit that shows you can cover losses. No loan, no interest. It's a small slice of the contract's value, and that's where the leverage comes from.

4

A small deposit, a big position

  • Gold at $2,000 an ounce, one contract of 100 ounces: about $200,000 of gold.
  • You might put up only around 5% of that, say $10,000, as margin.
  • So a 5% move in gold is a 100% swing on the money you actually put up.

That's why futures accounts move so fast, in both directions. The leverage is the same whether the move helps you or hurts you.

Marked to market, every single day

A futures trade isn't buy it and forget it. At the end of each session the exchange uses the settlement price to work out your gain or loss, and that cash is added to or taken out of your account. Whether you like it or not.

One gold contract, day by day

5

Initial margin, maintenance margin

  • Initial margin: the deposit you need to open the position. Your entry ticket.
  • Maintenance margin: a lower level your balance must stay above while you hold.
  • Fall below it and you get a margin call: deposit enough to get back up to the initial margin.

If you don't, or the market keeps running against you fast enough, the broker can close your position for you, whether you want it closed or not.

A futures trade is not a buy it and forget it kind of trade. — Martik Finance

Why it matters

  • Losses aren't on paper. They leave your account in real money each day the market moves against you.
  • With the made-up numbers above, a move of only 0.5% against you uses up the room between initial and maintenance.
  • Margin calls tend to come when the market is moving fastest, which is the worst time to be forced out.

The futures price isn't the spot price

The spot price is what the thing costs right now. A futures price is the market's expectation for a later date, plus the cost of carrying it until then: storage, interest rates and insurance for physical goods. Line up the prices for each expiry month and you get the futures curve.

Contango, backwardation, and negative oil

6

The day oil went negative

  • April 2020: lockdowns, and demand for oil collapsed almost overnight.
  • Tanks and pipelines filled up. There was nowhere left to put more oil.
  • The expiring contract was physically settled, so anyone still holding it would have to take real oil they had no way to store.

So holders paid someone else to take the contract. Oil wasn't worthless. The obligation to take delivery, with zero storage, was worse than worthless for a very short window.

Two shapes of the curve

later months higherContango

Common for goods that cost a lot to store. You pay up to own it later.

later months lowerBackwardation

High demand or a shortage right now. People pay more today than they expect to pay later.

The six risks to be honest about

Before you even think about placing a trade, know what you're exposed to.

Leverage cuts both ways

A small move against you is a large percentage loss on the money you put up.

5% against you can be 100% of your deposit.

Losses aren't capped

Unlike buying a stock outright, a leveraged futures position can lose more than your margin.

You can end up owing your broker.

Cash leaves daily

Mark to market means losses come out of your balance in real money each day.

Not theoretical. Not later.

Forced out at the worst time

Below maintenance you add funds right away, or the broker closes you.

Often right when it's most volatile.

Contracts expire

Forget the date on a physically settled contract and the goods are yours.

"Your 1,000 barrels are ready for pickup."

Thin contracts

Less popular contracts, or ones far from expiry, can have wide gaps between the price you want and the one you get.

You may not get out when you want.

Before your first futures trade

Five things to know about any contract before you touch it. Click through them.

1

Look up the contract

  • Contract size, tick size and tick value, from the exchange.
  • Work out what a 1-point move costs you per contract.

Crude: a $1 move is $1,000. E-mini S&P: a 1-point move is $50.

2

Know the margin

  • Initial margin to open, maintenance margin to hold.
  • Work out how far price can move against you before a margin call.

On the gold example, 0.5% against you is enough.

3

Size the risk in dollars

  • Stop distance in ticks × tick value × contracts = the dollars at risk.
  • Remember a fast market can blow through a stop. Losses can run past your margin.

If one contract is too big for your account, a micro contract is a tenth of the size.

4

Check the calendar

  • When does this contract expire?
  • Is it cash settled or physically delivered?

Close or roll well before expiration unless you really want the goods.

5

Trade where the volume is

  • Stick to liquid contracts, usually the front month of the big ones.
  • Thin or far-dated contracts can leave you stuck at a bad price.

In a fast or thin market, the exit you planned may not be there.

Questions

What is the difference between futures and options?

An option gives the buyer the right to buy or sell, and the most the buyer can lose is the premium. A futures contract is an obligation for both sides: unless you close it before it expires, you have to go through with it.

Is futures margin a loan?

No. In stocks, margin means borrowing from your broker and paying interest. In futures, margin is a security deposit that shows you can cover losses. There's no loan and no interest, and it's only a small slice of the contract's value.

What is a margin call in futures?

If daily losses push your balance below the maintenance margin, you have to deposit enough to bring it back up to the initial margin. If you don't, or the market keeps moving against you, the broker can close your position for you.

How much is one tick worth?

It depends on the contract. One crude oil contract is 1,000 barrels with a 1-cent tick, so a tick is $10 and a $1 move is $1,000. The E-mini S&P 500 is $50 times the index with a quarter-point tick worth $12.50.

Can you lose more than you put in with futures?

Yes. Because of leverage, a big enough move against you can lose more than your margin deposit, and you can end up owing your broker money. With 5% margin, a 5% move against you wipes out the whole deposit.

How did oil prices go negative in April 2020?

Demand collapsed and storage filled up. The expiring May crude contract was physically settled, so holders who couldn't take delivery paid others to take it. It settled at minus $37.63 on April 20, 2020, while later months stayed positive.

Small deposit, full-size risk.

Futures are useful tools for hedgers and speculators alike, and one of the easiest ways to turn a small market move into a large loss. The video's own advice: if you're watching to learn how they work, great. If you're about to open an account and start trading them, slow down first.

  • A futures contract is an obligation for both sides.
  • Know the contract size and tick value first.
  • Margin is a deposit; the leverage is real.
  • Your account settles in cash every day.
  • Know where the margin call is before you enter.
  • Close or roll before expiration.
