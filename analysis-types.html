Three Ways to Read a Market

Fundamental analysis asks what a company is really worth. Technical analysis asks what the price is doing. Macro analysis asks what the economy is doing to everything. Experienced investors use one, or a mix, instead of vibes.

What is the difference between fundamental, technical and macro analysis?

Fundamental analysis works out what a company is worth from its financial statements and qualities like its brand and leadership. Technical analysis reads the price chart: price, volume, trends, support and resistance. Macro analysis looks at the economy: interest rates, government spending, GDP, jobs and world events.

Which question are you asking?

Ever bought a stock because someone online said it was going to the moon, and it crashed right after? Guessing, copying and vibes aren't a method. Pick the question first; it tells you which kind of analysis answers it.

Fundamental analysis: what is it worth?

The most popular method for long-term investors. "I invest in companies, not stocks." As Warren Buffett put it, never invest in a business you cannot understand. You're working out what the company is actually worth, not just what the stock price says.

1

Is it strong, profitable, growing?

  • Quantitative: the numbers you can measure, straight from the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow report. How much money it makes, how much debt it carries, how well it invests in its future.
  • Qualitative: what you can't put a number on but still matters. Brand strength, leadership, competitive advantage, and whether its industry is growing or shrinking.

The video's Netflix example: subscriber growth slows and earnings soften, but the brand, the library and the push into new audiences still count. The numbers don't tell you everything.

Strengths and limits

strengthsThe company's real health

Built for long-term investing, not short-term swings. Real numbers like revenue, earnings and cash flow. The method top investors rely on.

limitsOpinions and forecasts

Qualitative calls are subjective. Predictions don't always come true. And companies can hide bad news: Enron looked solid until it fell apart.

Want the first two numbers to learn? EPS and the P/E ratio.

Technical analysis: what is price doing?

All about reading charts. Instead of financial reports or company news, technical traders study how the stock moves: its price, its volume, and the patterns those create.

Trend, levels, fakeout, breakout

2

The basic vocabulary

  • Volume is how many shares traded: how much energy is behind a move.
  • Uptrend: new highs, and pullbacks that don't fall as far. Downtrend: it keeps dropping and struggles to bounce.
  • Support is the floor where buyers jump in. Resistance is the ceiling where sellers take profits.
  • A break through either can start a new trend. A fakeout breaks, then snaps back and traps the early buyers.

Strengths and limits

strengthsVisual and fast

Relatively easy to follow, and it doesn't take long to get the hang of. No digging through reports.

limitsShows what, not why

Ignores fundamentals and news. Patterns don't always play out. It assumes history repeats, and new events can break that.

Macro analysis: what is the economy doing?

Zoom out to the whole economy: government policy, world events, inflation and the health of the economy.

3

Interest rates move everything

  • Central banks like the Federal Reserve run monetary policy, mainly by setting interest rates.
  • Low rates: borrowing is cheap, companies expand, people spend. That often pushes stocks higher.
  • Rising rates to fight inflation: borrowing slows, spending drops, and stocks usually take a hit.
  • Fiscal policy is government spending and taxes: more spending can push the economy short term; high taxes or cuts can slow it.

Macro suits broad investments like index funds and ETFs, because it shows which markets or regions might do well. It says nothing about a single company's earnings.

The indicators to know

GDP

How much the economy is growing. Growth is usually good news for stocks.

Unemployment rate

More people out of work means less spending and lower company earnings.

Consumer confidence

People who feel good about the economy spend more.

Manufacturing reports

Busy factories are a sign businesses are doing well.

Geopolitics

Wars, trade tensions, tariffs and even elections can shake markets fast.

Economic calendar

Free calendars, like investing.com's, list when each report comes out.

The three side by side

Each lens sees something the others miss. That's why many investors combine them.

Reading a chart, step by step

The technical toolkit from the video, in the order you'd use it. Click through it.

1

Start with price

  • Everything on the chart starts with the price moving up and down.
  • Ask where it is now compared with where it's been.

Price is the only thing every trader agrees on.

2

Check the volume

  • How many shares traded on the move.
  • Big moves on big volume carry more energy than quiet drifts.

Volume tells you how much conviction is behind price.

3

Name the trend

  • Higher highs and higher lows: an uptrend.
  • Lower highs and lower lows: a downtrend. Neither: sideways.

Stocks rarely move all over the place for no reason.

4

Mark support and resistance

  • The floor where buyers keep stepping in.
  • The ceiling where sellers keep taking profits.

These are where decisions get made.

5

Judge the break

  • A breakout closes through the level and keeps going.
  • A fakeout pokes through and snaps back, trapping early traders.

Don't jump in too soon: wait to see which one it is.

Questions

What is quantitative vs qualitative analysis?

Quantitative analysis uses numbers you can measure, from the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow report. Qualitative analysis judges what's hard to measure: brand strength, leadership, competitive advantage and industry trends.

What are support and resistance?

Support is a price floor where buyers tend to step in and the stock stops falling. Resistance is a ceiling where sellers tend to take profits and the stock stops rising.

What is a fakeout?

A move through support or resistance that quickly snaps back inside. It can trap traders who jumped in too soon, which is why many wait for a close beyond the level.

How do interest rates affect stocks?

Low rates make borrowing cheap, so companies expand and people spend, which often pushes stocks higher. Rising rates slow borrowing and spending, and stocks usually take a hit.

Which type of analysis is best?

Each has a blind spot. Fundamentals don't time entries, technicals don't explain why price moves, and macro says nothing about a single company. Many investors combine them.

Pick the question, then the lens.

Fundamental analysis tells you what you own. Technical analysis tells you when price agrees. Macro tells you what the tide is doing to every boat. Each one misses something, so most experienced investors use a mix.
