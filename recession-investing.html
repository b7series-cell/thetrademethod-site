What a recession actually is

A significant decline in economic activity that spreads across the economy and lasts more than a few months. This page won't teach you to predict the next one. Nobody can do that reliably.

1

Who calls it, and when

  • A common rule of thumb: two quarters in a row of falling GDP.
  • In the US the official call comes from the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which looks at jobs, income, industrial production and spending, not one number.
  • The NBER doesn't announce a recession when it starts. It announces it months later, once it's sure.

By the time you hear the word recession on the news, the economy has usually been in one for a while.

How long they last

About 17 months

The long-run average since the 1850s, skewed by old, ugly recessions before deposit insurance and active central banks.

About 10–11 months

The average since World War II.

18 months

2007 to 2009, one of the long, brutal ones.

2 months

The 2020 pandemic recession, officially over almost as soon as it began.

Recessions are a normal, repeating part of how economies work. You'll live through several more.

The official call comes late

Three mistakes that turn a drop into a loss

The drop is almost always temporary. What makes it permanent is selling at exactly the wrong moment.

1. Recency bias

Every recession feels uniquely terrifying. 2020: a pandemic, the economy shut down. 2008: the banks, the whole system broken. 2000: tech stocks were a fraud.

The story changes. The pattern doesn't.

2. Waiting for good news

"I'll get back in once it's safer" sounds reasonable. But the data confirming a recovery usually comes after the recovery has started.

By design, you miss the best days.

3. Economy = market

The market prices what investors expect months from now. Jobs and GDP reports tell you what already happened.

Bad news isn't the same as a bad time to invest.

March 2020: the worst and best days, side by side

Extreme uncertainty swings prices hard both ways. Panic selling causes the crashes, and any credible good news, a policy response or a rate cut, can cause an equally violent rally, often while the headlines are still terrible.

The 2020 crash and recovery

2

Same crash, two outcomes

  • The S&P 500 hit its low in March 2009. By early 2013 it had fully recovered, about four years from the bottom.
  • An investor who panic sold near that low and waited until things felt safe missed most of that recovery before getting back in.
  • Same crash, same world. The gap had nothing to do with the economy. It came from one decision.

The moment that feels most terrifying is often statistically close to some of the best days you could have stayed in for.

Forward-looking vs backward-looking

the marketLooks ahead

Prices what investors expect months from now, and starts pricing a recovery as soon as the outlook improves even slightly.

economic dataLooks back

Unemployment and GDP reports say what already happened. "Unemployment hits a new high" is old information.

Write the plan while you're calm

Across past recessions, the investors who came out ahead weren't the ones who called the bottom. Almost nobody does, including professionals. They were the ones who decided in advance what they'd do, then followed it mechanically.

3

Decide these today

  • What happens to your regular contributions if the market drops hard?
  • Any extra contributions, and at what drop?
  • Your emergency fund: how many months of expenses?
  • If you rebalance, the specific trigger: a percentage, a date, something concrete.

None of these require predicting anything. They require deciding once while you're thinking clearly, instead of over and over while you're scared.

Set your cash cushion

  • How many months of expenses you keep in cash, outside the market.

2

Keep your contributions going

  • Decide now that your regular amount continues when prices fall.
  • Falling prices mean the same money buys more.

Stopping in a panic is a decision too, just one made while scared.

3

Decide on extra, if any

  • Will you add more if the market drops hard? How much, and at what drop?
  • "Nothing extra" is a fine answer, if you decide it now.

A rule decided in advance doesn't need courage in the moment.

4

Pick a rebalancing trigger

  • A percentage your mix can drift, or a date on the calendar.
  • Something concrete, never "when it feels safe".

Feelings are the problem this plan exists to solve.

5

Write it down, then follow it

  • Keep the plan where you'll see it when the headlines get loud.
  • When panic hits, follow the list instead of improvising.

The winners in past recessions followed a plan; they didn't call the bottom.

Questions

Who decides when the US is in a recession?

The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER). It looks at jobs, income, industrial production and spending, and announces a recession months after it starts. A common rule of thumb is two quarters in a row of falling GDP.

How long do recessions last?

The US has had 34 since the 1850s, about 17 months on average, but only about 10 to 11 months on average since World War II. 2007 to 2009 lasted 18 months; the 2020 recession lasted 2.

Why do stocks recover before the economy?

The market looks ahead, pricing what investors expect months from now. Economic data like unemployment and GDP looks back. In 2020 the S&P 500 bottomed on March 23, and was already about 44% off its low when the recession was officially announced on June 8.

Should you wait for good news before investing again?

Waiting usually means missing the recovery, because the data confirming it arrives after it has started. In March 2020 some of the best days of the S&P 500 came within days of some of the worst.

What is recency bias in investing?

Giving too much weight to the crisis happening now and too little to how similar ones played out. Every recession feels like this time is different: 2000, 2008 and 2020 all did.

Decide once, while you're calm.

This is a page about long-term investing, not day trading, but the lesson carries: the drop is rarely what does the damage. The decision made in a panic is. Make your decisions before the next one, while you're thinking clearly.
