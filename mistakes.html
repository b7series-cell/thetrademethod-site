Watch one level, two markets

1

Trading the level without the bigger picture

  • A support level that's bounced before is still just an input variable — check what the broader market is doing before you trust it to bounce again.
  • On a day when the index is trending hard in one direction and everything is being sold, a support level either needs an almost surgical entry, or — more often — sets up a much better trade in the other direction: the break, not the bounce.

A level that held once is still just one data point. The market context around it is what tells you whether that data point is likely to repeat today.

2

Treating every level the same

  • The exact same price can mark two completely different levels depending on how price action responds to it. A level that gets tested and quickly rejected is not the same level as one that holds, closes above it, and does so on real volume.
  • Sizing every break of "the same" level identically ignores the quality of the setup each time, and turns your results into random chance instead of a read on your own skill.

The price being identical doesn't make the trade identical. What price action does around that price is the actual variable that matters.

3

Not looking left on the chart

  • A level that looks like "the low" on an intraday chart can sit right on top of a much more important level a little further away that never even got tested that session.
  • Shorting a breakdown without checking the daily or weekly chart for what's actually just below it can put you offside right into the real support — not through it.

The chart in front of you is only part of the story. The important levels from prior sessions are still there whether you look for them or not.

4

Ignoring volume at the level

  • A break of resistance on light or average volume is a much weaker signal than the same break with real participation behind it — no catalyst and no volume means no real reason to expect follow-through.
  • If you're buying a breakout, look for real volume pushing price through the level. If you're shorting a breakdown, look for the same on the way down. Without it, the fade is usually the better trade.

An important level should mean other participants are chomping at the bit to trade around it. If the volume isn't there, that's telling you the level isn't actually mattering to anyone yet.

5

Not planning what you'll do if the level actually works

  • Knowing you want to trade a level isn't the same as knowing what you'll do once it breaks, or once price reverts off it. Without that plan, you can be completely right about the level and still barely make any money.
  • Decide your plan before the trade: where the stop goes automatically, and whether you're staying with a break or taking a quick reversion — then actually follow it once you're in.

Being right about the level is only half the trade. What you do in the minutes after it works is usually where most of the actual money is made or left behind.

The level was never the problem.

Support and resistance work when you read the level correctly. Almost everything that actually goes wrong is one of these five correctable mistakes made trading around it — not the level itself.

  • Check the bigger picture before you trust a level to hold. The same level means something different in a trending market than a choppy one.
  • The price being identical doesn't make two trades identical. Read how price is actually behaving around the level.
  • Being right about the level is only half the trade — know your plan for what happens once it works, before you're in it.
