1 · Read price, not the tape

Raschke's case against reading order flow is simple. If buyers are really stepping in, you'll see price lift on a plain bar chart. The biggest players hide what they're doing, and the tape is full of noise from algorithms.

1

One watch

  • Her friend Richard Arms, who invented the Arms index, used to say: with one watch you always know what time it is. With two, you never do, because they never agree.
  • Moving averages and oscillators are built from the first and second derivatives of price. Price is always one step ahead of them.
  • Cut down to the few tools that actually help your pattern recognition.

For a model with a real statistical edge she says you'll do much better with ATR or percentage functions than with oscillators.

2

You can't see the big players

  • Big funds disguise their moves. They go through multiple accounts and multiple firms, and keep tweaking and adjusting. "If you think you can read that, you're crazy."
  • A boring mutual fund can take two weeks just to cut a large position in half.
  • That's your edge, not theirs: you can get in and out without slippage, and they can't.

Don't play a guessing game about who's doing what. Too many players, on too many time frames.

Three up bars say the same thing

"Wouldn't you just see the same thing on a bar chart?"

Lots of numbers to process while you also manage risk.

the bar chartThree bars up in a row

Even on a 5-minute chart, three consecutive up bars tell you there's an imbalance.

2 · The volume spike test

Her experiment for anyone who thinks they need the tape to see the big players. Take any intraday volume spike, drop down to the 1-minute chart, and see exactly where it came in.

Where did the volume come in?

1

How to run it

  • Find an intraday volume spike on a 5-minute chart. Gold, crude oil, bonds, currencies, index futures and copper all show them.
  • Drop to a 1-minute chart and find the exact bar where the volume came in.
  • Look left. Is that bar taking out a previous intraday swing high or swing low?

Her estimate: about 80% of the time, it is.

2

What it tells you

  • Lots of algorithms trigger off a channel high or a channel low getting penetrated. "It's so blatant and obvious."
  • The algo starts, price goes tick, tick, tick, and if it doesn't keep going by a set amount the algo goes flat so it doesn't get caught in a noise spike.
  • Off news reports the same thing happens even faster. That's a very dangerous time to get reactive.

The footprints show up at structural points you can mark in advance, on any chart.

Be grateful for the algos

They provide the liquidity and volume you need. And they open up what she calls opportunistic trading.

opportunisticWait in cash

Let one side get pulled offside, then step in and take advantage of it.

misguidedRead every ping

Algorithms running strategies that last 5 seconds to 5 hours. You can't out-read that from your chair.

Not too tight a time frame

"Look at a one minute stock chart. Tell me you can make sense of that." On too tight a time frame you're mostly in the noise. Use the 1-minute to check where a spike landed, not to hunt for trades.

3 · Get the main idea right

Most traders obsess over entries. Raschke starts with the day: is it going to be a low-to-high day or a high-to-low day? Get that right, she says, and that's 90% of the game.

Low-to-high day or high-to-low day?

1

Start with the day

  • Is there a trend for the day? Are we trading outside the first hour's range? "Trend for the day. That's your friend."
  • After two high-to-low days, the sellers have exhausted themselves: look for a low-to-high day. That's the rhythm from the Taylor trading technique she uses.
  • Then mind your initial trade location. Where you get in sets your risk.

The main idea first, the entry second.

2

Time-of-day habits

  • Breakout and range-expansion trades work best right off a session opening.
  • Crowds pay up too much off the open. In stocks you still see that opening bulge where everyone chases, and the move is over after the first 25 minutes.
  • On the floor, a counter-trend move came just before noon while traders went to lunch, and the afternoon could start the party again.

These tendencies come from human behavior, so they keep repeating.

Where the real money is

Aberrations: huge gaps, insane volume, huge bars, or a relationship moving beyond its historical norm, like 30-year yields dropping below 5%. They're rare and hard to model, and that's where the gold is buried. In the meantime: play the game, don't lose money, and when the opening comes, stay with it.

4 · Pick the exit like an actuary

Same entry, different exits, measured in average true range. Log which one would have been best on every trade and you're building an actuarial table, the way an insurance company works out life expectancy.

One entry, four ways out

1

Four exits, one ruler

  • Small target: half an ATR, on whatever time frame you trade, even a daily chart.
  • Large target: three ATRs, or a standard-deviation move.
  • Trail a stop: for example a volatility stop 2.5 ATRs from the extreme.
  • Time stop: hold for a set number of bars or days.

Most of the time, especially intraday, play for a small target, or a big one if the move is just starting.

2

Why the trail wins only about 15%

  • Everybody would like to trail a stop and catch the big move every time. On pure technical trading, she says, a trail is the best exit maybe 15% of the time.
  • Her team spent about two years modeling every trailing stop they could think of: parabolic, a moving average of the lows, Keltner and Bollinger bands, the chandelier stop, Wilder's volatility stop. One was best one quarter, another the next.
  • Her researcher's conclusion: on the best trades it doesn't matter which one you use. They all keep you in.

Easiest spots to trail: springs and traps, the false break that flushes everyone out and leaves a vacuum on the other side.

Breakout or fakeout?

You can't know for certain. Manage it instead: know before you enter how much room you'll give it, and what failure looks like.

  • Print the day's charts and circle where you got in and out.
  • Check each one: lunchtime with nobody around? A news event? A catalyst?
  • Measure how far the good trades went against you before they worked.
  • Turn that into a rule: a good trade shouldn't go beyond this point.

Not every breakout is a new high

One of her favorites is a three-bar coil: three overlapping daily bars where everybody agrees on price. When price pulls out of it, one side is on the wrong side. A breakout can also be the impulse out of a little pattern at the end of an ABC move.

5 · All in, all out

Raschke rarely scales out. If there's a reason to take part of the position off, she says, there's a reason to take the whole thing off.

Scaled winners, full-size losers

1

Why she rarely scales

  • Traders scale out on the winners, but when they get hit with a stop they're clipped on the whole thing. "That does not make sense to me."
  • She puts a position on and holds it until she's all out. Then it's on to the next shuffle.
  • If you do scale, Perry Kaufman's math says to take the first three of five pieces close together, because the odds of the move lasting drop off fast. Then the fourth, then a runner.

Her rule is the same either way: a reason to exit part is a reason to exit all.

Dollars per time unit

A phrase from a friend who traded volatility breakouts: the name of the game is making the most dollars in the least amount of time. Get in at structural points you can quantify, then let a target, a trail or a time stop decide when it's over.

6 · Bet the same every time

Raschke doesn't believe in varying her bet size. She doesn't know where the winners or the losers will be, and "the minute I start monkeying around with that, it's going to be Murphy's law."

10 corn contracts = 1 S&P

1

Never vary the bet

  • "High conviction" implies a cognitive bias. She'd rather not lean on it.
  • Every market gets the same bet, worked out from its average daily range in dollars.
  • She recalculates the unit at the start of every quarter, and the unit grows when the account does.

During the day she never thinks about dollars: "This is the spot on the game board. Put on a unit. This is the spot to take it off."

2

Know your weak spots

  • By her own account she's the worst at executing: she has bought the Swiss franc when it was supposed to be the S&P.
  • So for years an assistant pushed the buttons. She just said: buy one unit of silver.

Build your process around what you're bad at instead of pretending you'll fix it mid-trade.

7 · In a drawdown, cut size, unless it's a system

Two opposite schools, and both have sound math behind them. Which one applies depends on whether you're trading your judgment or a tested system.

Same streak, opposite rules

1

Discretionary: cut back

  • If you're a discretionary trader in a prolonged drawdown, something is wrong.
  • Maybe you're not getting enough sleep. Maybe there's been a regime change you haven't recognized. Maybe you're making unforced errors and getting sloppy. Maybe you should stop trading that market.
  • Cut back your size until you can regroup and figure out why the drawdown happened.

Find the cause, then go back to full size.

2

System: hold your size

  • A tested system's drawdowns are part of the plan.
  • Cutting size in a drawdown is the last thing you want to do, because that's when it's about ready to spring and have a big win again.

The mistake is mixing the two: cutting a system mid-drawdown, or pressing on through a discretionary slump.

When it starts to bother you

It shouldn't be that stressful. Under stress you start doing dumb things without noticing, so take a day or two off. And a 20% drawdown means very different things to someone whose account is a sliver of their net worth and someone with a mortgage and little cash.

8 · Play the game

Her fund ran four profit centers. In any given quarter a different one led, which gave her diversification she couldn't have planned. Click through them.

1

S&P day trading

  • Her base. She traded the S&P futures from the first day they listed.
  • One to three trades a day, trying to catch one or two of the 60-to-90-minute swings.
  • The edge came from knowing time-of-day behavior from her years as a floor trader.

"I know I can always go in and make a living S&P day trading."

2

In one day, out the next

  • Volatility breakouts and the two-day rhythm: after two high-to-low days, look for a low-to-high day.
  • Is there a trend for the day? Are we outside the first hour's range?
  • Profitable trades get held another day or two. Losers become day trades.

The main idea of the day, turned into an overnight trade.

3

Daily and weekly setups

  • The weekly chart corrects back down, and the daily shows a buy divergence at the end of that swing.
  • Look for the correction on the higher time frame and a little loss of momentum on the lower one. It works on any pair of time frames.

Not 100% of the time, but "best risk-reward in the book."

4

Seasonal trades

  • Some markets lean the same way at the same time of year. Her example: oats.
  • Grains have their own calendar of rollovers, crop reports and harvest seasonality.

Her book Trading Sardines is full of these stories.

routine

Homework the night before

Write out the game plan: half an hour. In the morning, look at the overnight action and the gaps, add a few notes, and let the day roll.

Phone in the kitchen, door closed.
signals

No trades off intuition

She's never made a trade off intuition. Every trade comes from a signal: a pattern or a condition.

What feels like a sixth sense is pattern recognition you've seen so many times you don't verbalize it.
flow

Single-minded, not anxious

Her best trading happens without thinking: up, test, reject, no volume up there, that was the top. You can't be in flow while you're anxious.

Flow is playing the game, whether you just won or just lost.
drill

The paper drill

Pick a simple volatility breakout system with a clear entry, exit and stop. Paper trade it in five markets, about two trades a week each, and write every result down.

Win, loss, win, loss: like flipping cards. It desensitizes you to both.
tools

AI for the grunt work

AI is a huge time saver for research, modeling, statistics and coding to your broker's API.

For finding patterns, she says a chatbot alone won't do it: that takes models trained on clean data.
longevity

Stay healthy, have fun

Exercise, sleep and energy are what keep you in the best game for the next 50 years.

"Energy is the most precious resource there is."
